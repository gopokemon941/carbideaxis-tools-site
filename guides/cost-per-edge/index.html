Carbide Insert Cost per Edge and Cost per Part

A lower price per insert does not always mean a lower insert cost per finished part. The comparison depends on the price per insert, the number of edges you can use, and the acceptable parts each edge produces on your job.

A lower price per insert does not always mean a lower insert cost per finished part. The comparison depends on the price per insert, the number of edges you can use, and the acceptable parts each edge produces on your job.

This guide calculates insert cost per acceptable part. It does not calculate the full cost of machining a part. Use the cost-per-edge calculator to compare your own inputs, and use a machining trial to check performance assumptions.

The formulas

Cost per edge = price per insert ÷ usable edges per insert

Insert cost per acceptable part = price per insert ÷ (usable edges per insert × average acceptable parts per edge)

Monthly insert spend = price per insert × inserts consumed per month

Keep the inputs consistent:

  • Enter the price of one insert, not one box. If the price is quoted per box, divide it by the number of inserts in that box.
  • Use the same currency and cost basis for both options. For example, decide whether the insert price includes shipping, then apply that basis consistently.
  • Count only parts that meet the agreed dimensional and surface-finish requirements.
  • Use positive values for usable edges and acceptable parts per edge. If either is unknown, the result is an estimate until measured.

For a completed trial, a useful cross-check is: total cost of inserts consumed ÷ total acceptable parts produced.

Count usable edges for the actual insert and job

The following are common nominal counts for conventional turning designs. They are starting points, not a guarantee that every edge is usable in your application.

Insert designation Common nominal cutting-edge count
CNMG, conventional double-sided design 4
WNMG, conventional double-sided design 6
TNMG, conventional double-sided design 6
SNMG, conventional double-sided design 8
CCMT, conventional single-sided design 2

Check the exact product specification and the corners accessible with your holder and operation. Damage or application restrictions can reduce the usable count. A negative clearance designation alone does not establish that an insert can be used on both sides.

The insert size chart helps with dimensions; size information alone does not confirm holder compatibility or the usable-edge count for a job.

Worked comparison: example numbers, not a quotation

The following hypothetical inputs demonstrate the arithmetic. They are not CarbideAxis prices, measured trial results or a promised saving. Both options are assumed to perform the same operation and meet the same part acceptance criteria.

Input or result Example A Example B
Price per insert $2.50 $2.00
Usable edges per insert 4 4
Average acceptable parts per edge 120 60
Acceptable parts per insert 480 240
Cost per edge $0.625 $0.500
Insert cost per acceptable part, approximately $0.00521 $0.00833
Insert cost per 1,000 acceptable parts, approximately $5.21 $8.33

For Example A, $2.50 ÷ (4 × 120) = $0.00520833… per acceptable part. For Example B, $2.00 ÷ (4 × 60) = $0.00833333… . Using the unrounded values, Example B has 60% higher insert cost per acceptable part despite its lower purchase price.

If you consume 300 inserts of Example A in a month, the insert spend is $750. Under these assumptions, they produce 144,000 acceptable parts. Producing the same number with Example B requires 600 inserts and costs $1,200. This is a fixed-output comparison, not a production forecast.

Measure parts per edge in a fair trial

  1. Record the setup. Note the full insert reference, holder, workpiece material, operation and relevant machine conditions.
  2. Define an acceptable part and the edge-change point. Agree dimensional tolerances, surface-finish requirements and when to stop using an edge before comparing results.
  3. Keep the comparison controlled. Use the same job and comparable material batches and conditions. Record any necessary changes so their effects are visible.
  4. Record each edge's results. Count acceptable parts and rejected parts separately. Note tool changes, dimensional drift, surface finish and the reason for replacing the edge.
  5. Use several observations. Report the range as well as the average. A single successful edge does not establish repeatable performance.

The required trial size depends on the job and its variation. Our minimum order for the covered standard turning models is 10 pieces per model; that commercial minimum is not a guarantee of sufficient test coverage. See the MOQ guide.

What this calculation includes

This calculation allocates the insert purchase cost across the acceptable parts produced. It does not separately include machine time, labour, tool-change downtime, holder cost, coolant, rejected material or rework. Shipping and taxes are included only if you include them in your insert-price input.

An insert that changes cycle time or scrap rate needs a broader comparison. Keep those results in your trial record before deciding which option has the lowest total machining cost.

Using the calculator and requesting a quotation

Use the calculator to work through measured inputs or clearly labelled what-if estimates. Keep estimated and measured results separate when comparing suppliers.

To discuss a result, choose an inquiry channel and review the details before sending. Sharing your calculation is optional; send only the information you want included in the quotation discussion.

For a trial quotation, send:

  • Your insert code, or clear photos for preliminary identification.
  • The workpiece material, operation and holder details.
  • The model quantities and delivery destination.
  • Optionally, measured parts per edge or a target insert cost per acceptable part.

You do not need to disclose your current supplier's price to start an inquiry. A target helps describe the trial objective; it is not a performance guarantee. Availability, specification, quantity, lead time and price are confirmed in the quotation.

Frequently asked questions

How many decimal places should I use?

Use enough precision to preserve a meaningful difference. In this example, five decimal places give approximately $0.00521 and $0.00833 per acceptable part. Showing cost per 1,000 acceptable parts is another readable option. Calculate percentage differences from unrounded values.

Which price should I enter if it changes with order quantity?

Use the price relevant to the quantity you intend to buy. You can compare trial and repeat-order scenarios separately. Label the quantity, currency and included charges for each scenario so a price-break difference is not mistaken for a performance difference.

Does a higher edge count always mean lower cost?

No. Price, usable-edge count and acceptable parts per edge all matter. Compare inserts suitable for the same job; a different shape is not automatically an interchangeable replacement.

Do I have to share my current supplier's price?

No. You can start a trial inquiry with the model, application, quantity and destination. Share cost or performance figures only if you want them considered in the discussion.

What if I do not know my parts per edge?

Measure it during a trial. You can also explore assumed values to see how sensitive the result is, provided you label them as estimates and do not present them as measured savings.

Is this the total cost of making a part?

No. It is the insert-cost component. Add the other relevant machining, quality and handling costs separately when making a purchasing decision.
