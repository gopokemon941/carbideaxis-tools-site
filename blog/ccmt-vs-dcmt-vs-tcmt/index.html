CCMT vs DCMT vs TCMT: Which Positive Turning Insert for Small Parts and Boring?

Three positive shapes, three different jobs: general small-part turning, reaching into profiles, and light internal work.

Have a turning insert code or holder photo?Check my turning insert

Choose CCMT when you need the strongest positive corner for general small-part turning, facing and boring. Choose DCMT when its narrower 55° point must reach into profiles, grooves or tight bore features. Choose TCMT when a triangular, three-edge positive insert fits your existing holder and light internal work.

All three codes share the same middle letters, and those letters are the family resemblance: C in second position is 7° clearance, which is what makes them positive, single-sided inserts; M is the tolerance class; T is a screw-down fixing hole with a single-sided chipbreaker form. What separates them is only the first letter — the shape — and this article is about what that one letter changes. If you want the whole code read position by position first, start with how to read an insert code.

What does the second letter C tell you about all three?

It tells you why these three families exist as a group, apart from the negative inserts most buyers know from CNMG and WNMG.

A second letter N means 0° clearance: the insert sides are square, the insert can be flipped and used on both sides, and the edge is strongly supported — but it pushes harder against the workpiece. A second letter C means the flank below the edge is relieved by 7°. The insert can only cut on one side, so you get fewer edges per insert, but the edge enters the material with less resistance and less radial push.

That trade — fewer edges for lower cutting force — is exactly what smaller work needs:

  • Small and benchtop lathes have less power and less rigidity to spare; a positive edge spends less of both.
  • Boring bars are long, slender and vibration-prone by construction; a lower-force edge is easier to keep quiet inside a bore.
  • Thin-walled and small-diameter parts deflect under push; a positive insert is less likely to bend the part away and spoil the size.

The cost is edge support. A positive corner is not the tool for heavy roughing or for hammering through a hard, interrupted surface — that is still negative-insert work. These three families are finishing-to-general tools for lighter, smaller, less rigid setups, and the choice among them is about shape, not about which is "stronger" in absolute terms.

Which one should you pick for general small-part turning and facing?

Start with CCMT.

The 80° corner is the strongest corner a positive insert offers. It has the most carbide behind the contact point, tolerates the small knocks and surface variations of everyday work best, and works in the most common positive setup of all: longitudinal turning and facing with the same tool, plus general boring in the smaller sizes. That is why, on our turning inserts page, CCMT is the positive family listed in three sizes while the others appear once or not at all — it is the positive default, the one a small shop stocks first.

Pick CCMT when:

  • one insert has to cover a mixed day of small shafts, bushings and faces rather than one specialised profile;
  • the job is boring a plain bore where no narrow feature dictates the shape;
  • the part is small but the cut itself is ordinary — no undercut, no tight internal shoulder, no copied contour.

Within CCMT, the size code does the rest of the work: the 06 size is the small end of the family for smaller bores and smaller machines, the 09T3 size is the general size most small-part work runs on. Both are in the size table below.

Which one reaches best into profiles and tight features?

DCMT — and this is the one job where CCMT cannot substitute for it.

A 55° corner is visibly narrower than an 80° corner. That pointed shape can follow a contour, turn down into a relief, copy a profile and reach internal features where the wider CCMT corner simply does not fit geometrically: the corner arrives at the wall of the feature before the edge reaches the surface it is supposed to cut. In our standard table DCMT appears exactly in that role — DCMT11T308 is listed as a finishing, profiling insert. If you run a negative system, the same 55° reach is covered in DNMG vs CNMG.

The trade is the mirror image of CCMT's strength. The 55° point is the least supported corner of the three, so it is a finishing and light general tool, not the insert to lean on. Use it where the shape of the part chooses it for you:

  • profiles and contours that must be copied rather than turned straight;
  • small grooves, reliefs and undercuts behind a shoulder;
  • internal features where the bore opens into a shaped section;
  • final passes where the corner must track a drawing, not just a diameter.

If none of those apply, the narrower point buys you nothing and costs you corner strength — go back to CCMT.

Which one should you pick for boring and internal turning?

Any of the three can bore; the bore itself decides which.

Inside a hole, two things matter that barely matter outside. First, clearance behind the edge: there is no open air behind the cutting point, so a relieved positive flank is what keeps the insert cutting instead of rubbing — one more reason internal work is positive-insert territory. Second, the shape must fit the bore's own shape: a plain cylindrical bore accepts the strong CCMT corner happily, while a bore that ends in a profile, a small shoulder or a shaped bottom may need the DCMT point to reach the last millimetres.

TCMT earns its place mainly here and in light general work on machines already equipped for it. The triangle gives three equal 60° corners on one side — one more usable edge per insert than the two rhombic shapes — at a corner angle between DCMT and CCMT for both strength and access. If your boring bars and holders are a TCMT system, that extra edge is a real economy. If you are starting from zero, most buyers are better served standardising on CCMT first and adding DCMT for profiles, simply because those are the positive sizes stocked and supported as standard, listed below.

One rule covers all three: the boring bar and its pocket are made for one shape. The insert follows the holder you have — or are willing to buy — never the other way round.

Does the material group change which shape you pick?

It changes the grade decision, not the shape decision — keep the two separate, as with every insert choice.

  • The shape (C, D or T, and the size and radius after it) is chosen by the part: its features, its bore, your holder and how rigid the setup is, as above.
  • The material group then decides the grade range: P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron, as set out in the workpiece material groups guide.

In our standard turning table these positive families are listed for P and M work, and that matches how they are used: positive inserts are the common answer for stainless (group M) on smaller lathes and in boring, where a sharper, lower-force edge helps with a material that sticks and work-hardens, and for general steel (group P) small parts. For cast iron (group K), buyers usually start from the negative 80° and square shapes instead, and treat a positive insert as a special case to confirm — if your job is in group K, say so in the inquiry and the match will be checked against the actual operation, not forced into this comparison.

Can you swap CCMT, DCMT and TCMT in the same holder?

No. Each shape seats in a pocket machined for that shape, held at the angle that shape's clearance is designed for. A DCMT will not seat in a CCMT pocket, a TCMT will not seat in either, and "the dimensions look close" does not change it — the DCMT and CCMT rows above share four dimension figures and still need different holders.

So treat a shape change as a holder change:

  1. 1. Check the holder or boring bar first. Its code tells you which family and size it takes. If you do not know how to read it, send a photo of the holder marking with your inquiry.
  2. 2. Change shape only for a geometric reason — a profile the 80° corner cannot reach, a bore feature that needs the 55° point — not because a different shape happened to be on the shelf.
  3. 3. Keep the trial honest. If you change shape, grade and chipbreaker in one step, you will not know what fixed (or broke) the result. Change the shape to solve the geometry, keep the material group the same, and score the outcome per edge.

For the listed CCMT and DCMT models, a trial starts from 10 pieces per model — one box of a single model, no mixing models to reach the minimum — so you can score a shape change on your own machine and material before committing shelf stock to it. That trial term is stated for the models in our standard table; for TCMT, send the code first and the supply option and terms will be confirmed for that specific code.

FAQ

What is the difference between CCMT and DCMT?

One letter and 25° of corner. CCMT is an 80° rhombic insert — the stronger, general-purpose positive shape for small-part turning, facing and plain boring. DCMT is a 55° rhombic insert — a narrower, weaker point that reaches into profiles, reliefs and tight internal features the 80° corner cannot follow. Both are 7°-clearance, single-sided, two-edge inserts.

How many usable edges does each insert have?

CCMT has 2, DCMT has 2 and TCMT has 3. All three are single-sided positive inserts, so only one face cuts. The rhombic shapes have two acute corners that do the turning work; the triangle has three equal 60° corners. None of them can be flipped for a second set of edges the way a negative insert can.

Are CCMT inserts good for boring?

Yes — CCMT is the most common positive boring choice in this group, and the smaller CCMT060204 size exists for smaller bores and machines. The 7° clearance keeps the flank from rubbing inside the hole and the 80° corner is the strongest positive option. For a bore that ends in a profile or tight feature, DCMT may reach what CCMT cannot.

Can I put a DCMT insert in a CCMT holder?

No. The pocket is machined for one shape, and the two inserts differ in shape and size code even where their IC, thickness and hole figures coincide. A shape change always means a matching holder or boring bar for that shape and size.

Which of the three has the strongest corner?

CCMT. Corner strength follows corner angle across these three: the 80° CCMT corner has the most support, the 60° TCMT corner is next, and the pointed 55° DCMT corner is the least supported. That is why DCMT is a finishing and profiling tool and CCMT carries the general work.

Which insert is best for profiling and copying a contour?

DCMT. Its 55° point follows contours, reliefs and undercuts that stop a wider corner, and in our standard range DCMT11T308 is listed precisely as a finishing, profiling insert. V-shape inserts at 35° reach further still, but that is a different family outside this comparison.

What does the letter C in the middle of all three codes mean?

It is the clearance class: 7° of flank relief, which makes the insert positive and single-sided. It is the shared reason all three cut with lower force than negative (N, 0°) inserts — and the shared reason they have fewer usable edges and less edge support. It says nothing about workpiece material.

Is TCMT part of your standard stocked turning range?

Not in the standard table. Our listed positive models are the CCMT sizes and DCMT11T308 shown above, listed for steel (P) and stainless (M). If you run TCMT, send the complete code, the chipbreaker suffix and grade from your box, and your holder model — the matching supply option will be checked for that code before anything is quoted.

The complete code for each model — including the chipbreaker suffix and the grade name from your current box if you have them — plus your holder or boring bar model, the workpiece material group (P, M or K) and whether the work is external, facing or boring. If the marking has worn off, send clear photos of the insert and holder instead of guessing the shape from memory.
