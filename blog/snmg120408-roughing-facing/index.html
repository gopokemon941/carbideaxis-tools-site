SNMG120408 for Roughing and Facing: When the Square Insert Beats CNMG

SNMG120408 — ANSI SNMG432 — is a square negative insert with eight usable edges and the strongest corner in our turning range: a 90° included angle, against 80° on a CNMG and 55° on a DNMG. It is the insert for taking material off fast — roughing, heavy facing and scaled or interrupted surfaces — and it is not a general-purpose insert. Use it to rough, then finish with a CNMG or a DNMG. Check the holder first, trial one box of ten.

The three inserts in this table share the same inscribed circle, thickness and hole — and the same ANSI size number, 432. What changes is the shape, and the shape decides both the edge count and the strength of the corner. The square brings four corners to each face, so 4 × 2 = 8 edges, the most in our turning range. The same square shape gives the 90° corner, the bluntest and strongest corner of the three. The shape-by-shape comparison behind this table is covered in CNMG vs WNMG.

How do you read SNMG120408?

Letter by letter, the same way as every ISO turning code — the full decoding is in how to read an insert code, and this is the short version for this one code:

  • S — the shape: a square, four cutting corners per side, with a 90° included angle at each corner.
  • N — the clearance: 0°, a negative insert. It sits tilted in a negative holder and can be used on both faces.
  • M — the tolerance class.
  • G — the fixing and chipbreaker style: a hole through the insert, with a chipbreaker on both faces.
  • 12 — the inscribed circle size: 12.7 mm.
  • 04 — the thickness: 4.76 mm.
  • 08 — the nose radius: 0.8 mm.

Read the last two digits carefully — they are the ones buyers mix up. SNMG120408 ends in 08, a 0.8 mm nose radius for general roughing. SNMG120404 ends in 04, a 0.4 mm nose radius for lighter roughing and a finer finish after the heavy cut. Same shape, same eight edges, different corner. If your current box says SNMG431, that is the 0.4 mm version; SNMG432 is the 0.8 mm version in this article.

Why is the square corner the strongest?

Because a 90° corner has the most carbide behind the point that takes the cut.

A cutting corner is a wedge, and the wider the wedge, the more material supports its tip. A square corner is 90°; a CNMG or WNMG corner is 80°; a DNMG corner is 55°; a VNMG corner is 35°. As the angle narrows, the point gets sharper and reaches further into profiles — and weaker. The square gives up reach and takes strength instead. That is the whole trade of the SNMG: it cannot do what a sharp diamond does, but where the cut is heavy, the surface is scaled, or the edge keeps entering and leaving the material, the square corner survives where a sharper corner chips.

The edge count follows from the same geometry. A negative insert is flat on both faces — 0° clearance means there is no top and bottom in the cutting sense — so flipping it over gives a fresh set of corners. A square brings four corners to each face, so 4 × 2 = 8 usable edges. A CNMG diamond brings two usable corners per face, so 2 × 2 = 4. A WNMG trigon brings three per face, so 3 × 2 = 6. The rake system behind the edge count is explained in negative vs positive inserts.

When does the square insert actually pay?

When the job is to remove a lot of material before the finishing insert ever touches the part.

The SNMG pays best on roughing: turning down a forging, a casting or a bar to near size, facing a large diameter flat, and cutting through scale on cast iron or a hard skin on a rough surface. It also pays on interrupted cuts — a keyway, a hole or a flat that breaks the cut — where the edge slams into the material again on every revolution and a weaker corner would chip. In all of these, the square corner's strength is the point: the insert keeps cutting through the rough work instead of being changed out, and its eight edges mean one box carries 80 edges against 40 from a box of ten CNMG inserts — twice as many edges per box, on the same size class.

It pays in steel (P) and cast iron (K) above all, the two material groups where roughing volume is highest, and it pays on rigid machines with solid workholding, where the machine can push the cut the insert is built for. It pays least where the part is already near size — there the heavy corner is the wrong tool, and the finishing insert should be in the holder already.

When is SNMG the wrong choice?

Whenever the corner has to follow a shape, or the machine cannot push a square edge.

The 90° corner cannot trace a profile. It cannot copy a shoulder, a taper or a detailed contour the way a 55° DNMG can, and it cannot reach into a tight feature the way a 35° VNMG can. On a profiled part, the square leaves steps where the shape should be smooth, and no change of feed will fix that — the corner geometry is the limit. The profiling comparison is covered in DNMG vs CNMG.

The second limit is force. A square negative edge takes the heaviest cut of the negative family, and a small or less rigid lathe may chatter or deflect where a CNMG would still cut, and where a positive insert such as CCMT would cut cleanly. Thin-walled parts and long overhangs are the same story: the part, not the insert, becomes the weak link. And the SNMG needs its own holder pocket: the same inscribed circle as a CNMG does not mean the same holder, because the square and the diamond seat differently. Changing shape means checking the holder, not just the box.

The third limit is finish. A roughing insert is built to remove material, not to leave a fine surface. The 0.8 mm nose and the heavy cut leave feed marks that a finishing pass must remove. That is not a defect — it is the division of labour. Rough with the SNMG, finish with the CNMG120404 or a DNMG, and do not judge the roughing insert by the surface it was never meant to leave.

How does SNMG120408 compare with CNMG120408?

They are the same size and the same system, and the decision between them is about the job, not the insert.

Both are negative, double-sided, 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 4.76 mm thick, 5.16 mm hole, 0.8 mm nose radius in the 08 versions, and both belong to our regularly stocked turning range. The differences are the shape and what follows from it: the SNMG square gives eight edges against the CNMG diamond's four, and a 90° corner against the CNMG's 80°. On heavy roughing the square is the stronger, longer-lasting choice; on general turning, profiling and finishing, the CNMG's diamond reaches further and leaves a better surface. Most shops that run both keep them for different operations on the same part — the SNMG takes the part to near size, the CNMG finishes it — rather than choosing one and discarding the other. The same question asked from the six-edge side is covered in the WNMG080408 guide.

So the practical rule is simple: if the cut is heavy, the surface is scaled, or the edge keeps breaking in and out of the material, put the square insert in; if the part is near size, profiled, or the machine is small, keep the CNMG in. The dimension tables for both families are in the insert size chart.

What does it cost per edge?

Less per edge than any other insert in our turning range on the maths alone — and that is the only honest way to say it without inventing a price.

No price is quoted in this article, because our prices are quoted per job after the chipbreaker is confirmed. What can be said without a price is the ratio: eight edges against four is twice as many edges per insert, so at any given piece price the SNMG's cost per edge is half the CNMG's, and three-quarters of the WNMG's six-edge cost. That ratio is what the cost-per-edge calculator is for — put in the piece price you were quoted for each shape and it returns the per-edge cost, the monthly consumption and the comparison in one place. Two warnings from the same maths: never compare an SNMG and a CNMG by piece price alone, because the edge counts differ by a factor of two; and never buy on edge count alone either, because a roughing edge under a heavy cut wears differently from a general turning edge — eight edges only save money if the corners survive the roughing work they were bought for, which is exactly what a trial box is for.

FAQ

What does SNMG120408 mean? S is the square shape with a 90° included angle, N is 0° clearance (negative, double-sided), M is the tolerance class, G is the hole and double-sided chipbreaker style, 12 is a 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 04 is 4.76 mm thickness, and the final 08 is a 0.8 mm nose radius. Its ANSI equivalent is SNMG432.

How many cutting edges does SNMG120408 have? Eight — four corners per face, usable on both faces because it is a negative insert. A CNMG120408 of the same size has four, a WNMG080408 has six, and a DNMG150408 has four.

What is the difference between SNMG120408 and SNMG120404? Only the nose radius: 0.8 mm against 0.4 mm. The 0.8 mm version (ANSI SNMG432) is the general roughing corner; the 0.4 mm version (ANSI SNMG431) is for lighter roughing and a finer finish after the heavy cut. Both have eight edges.

Is SNMG120408 the same size as CNMG120408? In inscribed circle, thickness and hole, yes: 12.7 mm, 4.76 mm and 5.16 mm, both ANSI size 432. But they are different shapes — square against diamond — so they need different holder pockets and are not interchangeable in the same holder.

Why is SNMG the cheapest per edge in the turning range? Because the cost of one insert is spread over eight edges instead of four — twice as many edges per insert as a CNMG. At any piece price, the SNMG's cost per edge is half the CNMG's. Run both through the cost-per-edge calculator with your quoted prices to see it for your numbers.

Can SNMG do profiling work? Not well. The 90° square corner cannot follow a contour the way a 55° DNMG or a 35° VNMG can, and it leaves steps on shoulders and tapers. Use SNMG for straight roughing and facing, and choose the sharper diamond where the corner must follow the profile.

Can I use SNMG120408 on a small lathe? Often not well. Like all negative inserts it needs cutting force, and the square edge needs the most of the family — a small or less rigid lathe may chatter or deflect. On small machines, long boring bars and thin-walled parts, a CNMG or a positive insert such as CCMT is usually the better fit.

What is the minimum order for SNMG120408? 10 pieces of the one model — one box, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. Trial orders run 7–15 days.

The holder matters here, because the square needs its own pocket, and the chipbreaker is confirmed manually for your job before we quote.
