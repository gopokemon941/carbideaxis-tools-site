WNMG080408 Guide: Six Edges and What They Save You

WNMG080408 — ANSI WNMG432 — is an 80° trigon insert with six usable edges, two more than a CNMG120408 of the same size. On a rigid lathe, on general external turning and facing, those two extra edges are the whole story: the insert does more work before the box runs out. Check the holder first, trial one box of ten.

The three inserts in this table share the same inscribed circle, thickness and hole — and the same ANSI size number, 432. What changes is the shape, and the shape decides the edge count. That is why the WNMG is the one to understand first if your shop turns the same parts in volume: same size class, half as many edges again. How do you read WNMG080408?

Letter by letter, the same way as every ISO turning code — the full decoding is in how to read an insert code, and this is the short version for this one code:

  • W — the shape: an 80° trigon, three cutting corners per side.
  • N — the clearance: 0°, a negative insert. It sits tilted in a negative holder and can be used on both faces.
  • M — the tolerance class.
  • G — the fixing and chipbreaker style: a hole through the insert, with a chipbreaker on both faces.
  • 08 — the inscribed circle size: 12.7 mm.
  • 04 — the thickness: 4.76 mm.
  • 08 — the nose radius: 0.8 mm.

Read the last two digits carefully — they are the ones buyers mix up. WNMG080408 ends in 08, a 0.8 mm nose radius for general work. WNMG080404 ends in 04, a 0.4 mm nose radius for lighter cuts and finer finishing. Same shape, same six edges, different corner. If your current box says WNMG431, that is the 0.4 mm version; WNMG432 is the 0.8 mm version in this article.

Why does WNMG have six edges?

Because a trigon has three corners per side, and a negative insert has two sides.

A negative insert is flat on both faces — 0° clearance means there is no top and bottom in the cutting sense — so flipping it over gives a fresh set of corners. A trigon brings three corners to each face, so 3 × 2 = 6 usable edges. A CNMG diamond brings two corners per face, so 2 × 2 = 4. An SNMG square brings four per face, so 4 × 2 = 8 — the most edges in our turning range, but on a square insert built for roughing and facing, not a like-for-like general turning shape. Among general turning shapes of this size, the WNMG's six edges are the most you get per insert.

This is the economics of the whole negative family, and the WNMG is where it shows most clearly: the price of the insert is spread over more edges, so the cost per edge falls even when the price per piece does not. Work it out per edge, not per piece, with the cost-per-edge calculator — and compare it against the CNMG you run now on the same maths before deciding on piece price. The rake system behind the edge count is explained in negative vs positive inserts.

When does the six-edge advantage actually pay?

When the same insert stays in the holder and keeps cutting — volume general turning on a rigid lathe.

The advantage is not in any single cut. A WNMG corner cuts like a CNMG corner of the same nose radius: same 0.8 mm radius, same 80° included angle, same negative edge character. The advantage is that the box lasts longer. A box of ten WNMG inserts gives you 60 nominal 80° cutting corners. A box of ten CNMG inserts gives you 40. Each shape needs its own compatible holder pocket. For a fair comparison, run the same job with a suitable holder for each insert and record how many acceptable parts each edge produces. Use that result to judge the cost per part. On a line that turns the same steel parts shift after shift, that is fewer box changes, fewer reorders and fewer interruptions for the same output.

It pays best where the work is steady external turning and facing: shafts, flanges, sleeves and general parts in steel (P), stainless steel (M) and cast iron (K), on a machine rigid enough for a negative insert. It pays least where the job changes every hour — there the edge count never gets the chance to accumulate, and the shape of the corner matters more than how many of them there are.

When is WNMG the wrong choice?

Whenever the corner cannot reach the feature, or the machine cannot push a negative edge.

The 80° trigon is a strong, blunt corner. It is excellent for straight turning and facing, and poor for getting into tight profiles: it cannot trace a shape the way a 55° DNMG can, and it cannot copy a fine contour the way a 35° VNMG can. If the part has shoulders, undercuts or detailed profiles that the corner must follow, the WNMG's strength becomes a limitation — the shape that gives it six edges is the same shape that keeps it out of the corner. The profiling comparison is covered in DNMG vs CNMG, which is the same question asked from the 55° side.

The second limit is the machine. A negative insert needs cutting force, and a small or less rigid lathe may chatter or deflect where a positive insert would cut cleanly. If the machine is the weak link, the right answer is a positive insert — CCMT or DCMT — not a different negative shape. And the WNMG needs its own holder pocket: the same inscribed circle as a CNMG does not mean the same holder, because the trigon and the diamond seat differently. Changing shape means checking the holder, not just the box.

How does WNMG080408 compare with CNMG120408?

They are the same size and the same system, and the decision between them is almost entirely about edges.

Both are negative, double-sided, 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 4.76 mm thick, 5.16 mm hole, 0.8 mm nose radius in the 08 versions, and both belong to our regularly stocked turning range. The differences are the shape and what follows from it: the WNMG trigon gives six edges against the CNMG diamond's four, and the trigon's three corners give a slightly different approach to facing cuts, where the extra corner can be an advantage on facing work. The CNMG's diamond shape, with its two corners per side, is the more familiar general-purpose geometry and the one most shops already hold in stock — which is a real reason to keep it, because a holder, a stock of inserts and an operator's habit are all part of the cost of changing.

So the practical rule is simple: if you already run CNMG and the work is steady volume turning, trial one box of WNMG080408 in its proper holder and count the edges you actually use; if the work is varied, profiled, or the CNMG is already doing the job with no complaints, there is nothing to fix. The full side-by-side is in CNMG vs WNMG, and the dimension tables for both families are in the insert size chart.

What does it cost per edge?

Less per edge than the same-size CNMG on the same maths — and that is the only honest way to say it without inventing a price.

No price is quoted in this article, because our prices are quoted per job after the chipbreaker is confirmed. What can be said without a price is the ratio: six edges against four is 50% more cutting edges per insert, so at any given piece price the WNMG's cost per edge is two-thirds of the CNMG's. That ratio is what the cost-per-edge calculator is for — put in the piece price you were quoted for each shape and it returns the per-edge cost, the monthly consumption and the comparison in one place. Two warnings from the same maths: never compare a WNMG and a CNMG by piece price alone, because the edge counts differ; and never compare either against a positive insert by piece price, because a CCMT has only two edges and the comparison is off by a factor of three.

FAQ

What does WNMG080408 mean? W is the 80° trigon shape, N is 0° clearance (negative, double-sided), M is the tolerance class, G is the hole and double-sided chipbreaker style, 08 is a 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 04 is 4.76 mm thickness, and the final 08 is a 0.8 mm nose radius. Its ANSI equivalent is WNMG432.

How many cutting edges does WNMG080408 have? Six — three corners per face, usable on both faces because it is a negative insert. A CNMG120408 of the same size has four, a DNMG150408 has four, and an SNMG square has eight.

What is the difference between WNMG080408 and WNMG080404? Only the nose radius: 0.8 mm against 0.4 mm. The 0.8 mm version (ANSI WNMG432) is the general-purpose corner; the 0.4 mm version (ANSI WNMG431) is for lighter cuts and finer finishing. Both have six edges.

Is WNMG080408 the same size as CNMG120408? In inscribed circle, thickness and hole, yes: 12.7 mm, 4.76 mm and 5.16 mm, both ANSI size 432. But they are different shapes — trigon against diamond — so they need different holder pockets and are not interchangeable in the same holder.

Why is WNMG cheaper per edge than CNMG? Because the cost of one insert is spread over six edges instead of four — 50% more edges per insert. At any piece price, the WNMG's cost per edge is two-thirds of the CNMG's. Run both through the cost-per-edge calculator with your quoted prices to see it for your numbers.

Can WNMG do profiling work? Not well. The 80° trigon corner cannot reach into tight profiles the way a 55° DNMG or a 35° VNMG can. Use WNMG for straight turning and facing, and choose the sharper diamond where the corner must follow the profile.

Can I use WNMG080408 on a small lathe? Often not well. Like all negative inserts it needs cutting force, and a small or less rigid lathe may chatter or deflect. On small machines, long boring bars and thin-walled parts, a positive insert such as CCMT or DCMT is usually the better fit — trial one box and compare the finish.

What is the minimum order for WNMG080408? 10 pieces of the one model — one box, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. Trial orders run 7–15 days.

What should I include in a quote request for WNMG080408? The full code — WNMG080408, or WNMG080404 if you want the 0.4 mm corner — plus your holder model, and the chipbreaker suffix and grade name from your current box if you have them, or at minimum the material group (P, M or K) and the operation. The holder matters here, because the trigon needs its own pocket.
