CNMG120408 vs CNMG120404: 0.8 mm or 0.4 mm Nose Radius — Which One Should You Choose?

Only the last two digits change — but they decide edge strength, finish and cutting force. Here is how to pick between the 0.8 mm and 0.4 mm corner.

Have a turning insert code or holder photo?Check my turning insert

Choose CNMG120408 (0.8 mm nose radius) for stronger edges, general and heavier turning, and better tolerance of interrupted cuts. Choose CNMG120404 (0.4 mm) for finer finish, lower cutting force, and slim or vibration-prone parts. Both share the same CNMG1204 body and fit the same holder — only the corner changes.

Everything above the last row is ISO geometry: the two codes are the same insert in the same size, and only the nose radius digit changes. If you are still learning to read the full code position by position, start with how to read an insert code — this article assumes you already have the right CNMG1204 size and are only deciding the corner.

What changes when only the last two digits change?

The nose radius is the small arc at the tip of the 80° corner — the part that forms the finished surface and takes the highest load at the point of contact.

A 0.8 mm radius puts more carbide behind the contact point. The corner is stronger, heat and load are spread over a larger arc, and the edge tolerates a less perfect cut — scale, a hard spot, or a cut that enters and leaves the material — better than a sharper corner does.

A 0.4 mm radius concentrates the cut on a smaller arc. The corner is less supported, but the insert pushes less against the workpiece, follows small corner details more closely, and leaves a smaller fillet where a turned diameter meets a shoulder.

Neither is an upgrade of the other. They are two corners on the same body, for two different priorities: edge strength on one side, surface and force control on the other. The full CNMG family dimensions, including both codes side by side, are listed on the CNMG120408 family page and in the insert size chart.

Which one should you pick for roughing and general turning?

Start with CNMG120408.

For roughing and general external turning on rigid parts, the limiting factor is usually the corner itself: a weak corner chips, and a chipped corner ends the edge long before normal wear does. The 0.8 mm radius is the safer default here — it is the standard general-purpose choice in the CNMG1204 size, and it is the one to trial first when the cut is interrupted, the surface is scaled or uneven, or the same insert has to cover a mixed day of jobs rather than one optimised operation.

This is also the practical default for stocking: if a distributor or a job shop can only keep one of the two on the shelf for general work, the 08 covers the wider range of everyday turning. Buyers building that shelf stock can see the wider range on the turning inserts page.

Picking the 08 does not mean picking the heaviest cut the machine can take. It means that when something in the cut is imperfect, the corner is more likely to survive it.

Which one should you pick for finishing and a finer surface?

Start with CNMG120404 when the surface is the specification, not just the result.

Because the 0.4 mm corner contacts the workpiece over a smaller arc, it generates less radial push for the same job and can produce a finer finish in a light finishing pass than the larger radius. It is the common choice when:

  • the drawing calls for a fine finish that the 08 is not reaching cleanly;
  • the part must keep a small corner fillet at a shoulder, which a 0.8 mm corner cannot form;
  • the final pass is a light sizing pass rather than a stock-removal pass.

The trade is durability: the sharper corner has less material supporting it, so it is less forgiving of interruptions, scale and hard inclusions. Using a 04 as a roughing insert because it happens to be in the holder is one of the most common reasons a finishing insert gets blamed for chipping — the corner was never the problem, the job assigned to it was.

What if your part is slim, thin-walled or the machine is small?

This is where the 04 often wins even outside finishing.

A larger nose radius increases the radial component of the cutting force — the push that bends a long, slim shaft away from the tool, flexes a thin wall, or sets up vibration on a lighter machine. On those parts the stronger corner can work against you: the edge survives, but the part deflects or chatters and the surface and size suffer.

The 0.4 mm radius lowers that push. If you are seeing chatter marks, size that drifts along a long shaft, or a finish that gets worse rather than better with the 08, trialling the 04 in the same holder is a legitimate diagnostic step, not a downgrade — it separates a force-and-rigidity problem from a grade or chipbreaker problem.

The reverse also holds: on a short, rigid part in a powerful machine, that extra force is rarely the limiting factor, and giving up the stronger 08 corner buys you nothing.

Does the right radius depend on steel, stainless or cast iron?

The radius choice and the material choice are two separate decisions — do not let one hide the other.

  • The radius (04 or 08) decides corner strength, cutting force and the surface the corner can form.
  • The material group — P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron — decides which grade range the insert should come from. The background is explained in the workpiece material groups guide.
  • The chipbreaker, named by the manufacturer-specific suffix after the ISO code, decides how the chip forms and breaks in that material.

So a buyer turning stainless (group M) still has to choose 04 or 08 on the same logic above — finishing and slim parts point to 04, general and interrupted work points to 08 — and then have the grade and chipbreaker matched to group M. At CarbideAxis that chipbreaker and grade match is confirmed manually, job by job, against your workpiece material and operation before we quote; it is never picked from a table and never assumed from the ISO code alone. If the code on your box is incomplete, the insert code guide shows which parts you still need to supply.

Can you swap 04 and 08 in the same holder?

Yes — that is the convenience of this comparison. Both codes share the same inscribed circle, thickness and fixing hole, so both seat in the same CNMG 12-size holder. No holder change is needed to trial the other radius.

Three checks still apply before you treat a swap as a fair trial:

  1. 1. Corner fillet on the drawing. Can the part accept the larger fillet an 08 corner leaves at shoulders? If not, the 04 is not optional, whatever the edge-life result says.
  2. 2. Same grade group and chipbreaker intent. Compare radius against radius. If you change the supplier, grade or chipbreaker at the same time, you will not know which change caused the result.
  3. 3. Same workpiece and operation. Score the trial the way you would score any insert trial: same part, same operation, count the outcome per edge.

Both codes belong to the CNMG family within our regularly stocked turning range, and a trial starts from 10 pieces per model — one box of a single model, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. That lets you trial the 04 against the 08 you already run, one box at a time.

FAQ

Is CNMG120408 stronger than CNMG120404?

At the corner, yes. The 0.8 mm radius puts more carbide behind the contact point, so it tolerates heavier general turning and interrupted cuts better. The rest of the insert — body, thickness and hole — is identical, so the difference is corner strength only, not a different class of insert.

Does CNMG120404 always give a better finish than CNMG120408?

Not always — it gives you the better chance of a finer finish in a light finishing pass, because the smaller corner pushes less and follows the surface over a smaller arc. On a rigid part in good conditions, an 08 can still finish well. The 04 earns its place when finish, small fillets or low force are the actual problem.

Can CNMG120404 replace CNMG120408 in the same holder?

Yes. Both are CNMG1204 size — 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 4.76 mm thick, 5.16 mm hole — so both fit the same holder. Before you swap, check that the part drawing accepts the corner fillet of the radius you are moving to, and keep the grade group and chipbreaker the same so the trial isolates the radius.

Which radius is better for interrupted cuts?

CNMG120408. An interrupted cut repeatedly impacts the corner as it enters the material, and the larger, better-supported 0.8 mm corner absorbs that impact better than a 0.4 mm corner. Using a 04 for interrupted work usually shortens corner life even when the grade is right.

Which radius should I use for a long, thin shaft?

Usually CNMG120404. A slim shaft deflects under radial cutting force, and the smaller radius pushes less, which reduces deflection and chatter risk. If the same job also includes a roughing stage on a rigid section, many shops rough with the 08 and finish the slim section with the 04.

What fillet will each radius leave at a shoulder?

The insert cannot form an internal corner fillet smaller than its own nose radius. A CNMG120404 can form a fillet down to its 0.4 mm corner, while a CNMG120408 will leave at least its 0.8 mm corner. If the drawing specifies the smaller fillet, the 04 is required regardless of edge-life preference.

Does the nose radius change how many usable edges I get?

No. Both are double-sided negative CNMG inserts with 4 usable edges. Radius changes corner behaviour, not edge count — edge count is set by the shape and clearance letters earlier in the code.

I do not know which radius I am running now. How do I check?

Read the last two digits of the code on the box or the insert marking: 04 is 0.4 mm, 08 is 0.8 mm. If the marking has worn off, the radius is hard to judge reliably by eye — send a clear photo of the insert instead of guessing, and it can be identified from the insert as a whole.

What should I include in a quote request for these two codes?

The full code for each model you want priced — CNMG120408 or CNMG120404 — plus the chipbreaker suffix and grade name from your current box if you have them, or at minimum the material group (P, M or K) and the operation (roughing, general or finishing). Quotes are only comparable when the radius, chipbreaker and grade group all match the request.
