DNMG vs CNMG: 55° or 80° — Which Turning Insert Should You Choose?

Both are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges — the corner angle is what sends each one to different work.

Have a turning insert code or holder photo?Check my turning insert

Choose CNMG (80° corner) for stronger corners, general and heavier external turning, and facing. Choose DNMG (55° corner) when access matters more — profiling, copying contours and reaching closer to shoulders and details. Both are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges, but they fit different holders and are not interchangeable.

Two things in this table surprise many buyers. First, the edge count is the same: narrowing the corner from 80° to 55° does not cost you edges — both shapes are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges. Second, the two examples share the same inscribed circle and fixing hole, yet they still do not fit the same holder, because the shape and thickness differ. Shape is the first letter of the code for a reason; if you are still reading codes position by position, start with how to read an insert code.

What does the corner angle actually change?

The corner angle is the included angle at the working tip of the rhombic insert — 80° on a C shape, 55° on a D shape. Everything else in this comparison follows from that one geometric difference.

A wider corner puts more carbide behind the cutting point. The tip is supported on both sides by more material, the load spreads over a stronger wedge, and the corner tolerates a less perfect cut — scale, hard spots, interruptions — better.

A narrower corner puts less carbide behind the point, so the tip is weaker. In exchange, the slim point can enter places a wide corner physically cannot: it can follow a contour, turn closer into a shoulder or a detail, and complete profiles that would force a tool change with an 80° insert.

Neither shape is an upgrade of the other. CNMG trades access for strength; DNMG trades strength for access. The full dimension tables for both families are listed in the insert size chart, and both sit inside the range on the turning inserts page.

Which one is stronger, and which jobs does that suit?

CNMG, clearly — and that is why it is the default starting point for most external turning.

For roughing and general turning on rigid parts, for heavier cuts, and for interrupted surfaces, the limiting factor is usually the corner itself. A corner that chips ends the edge long before normal wear does, and the 80° corner survives conditions that would damage a 55° tip. CNMG is also the natural choice for facing work: the wide corner and the holders built around it are arranged for turning and facing with the same setup, which is why so many general-purpose lathe stations are built around CNMG120408 — the family dimensions are on the CNMG120408 family page.

This is also the practical stocking default. If a distributor or job shop keeps only one of the two shapes for everyday work, CNMG covers the wider range of ordinary turning. That general-purpose role is exactly what it is compared on in CNMG vs WNMG: CNMG is the 4-edge strength option in that pairing too.

Choosing the stronger shape does not mean choosing the heaviest cut the machine can take. It means that when something in the cut is imperfect, the corner is more likely to survive it.

Which one reaches better — profiling, contours and shoulders?

DNMG, and this is the whole reason the shape exists.

A 55° point can profile shapes that an 80° corner cannot follow: contoured surfaces, transitions into shoulders, smaller details and finishing passes where the tool has to track a drawn profile rather than simply remove stock in straight lines. Where a CNMG would bridge over a detail or force a second tool, a DNMG often completes the profile in the same setup.

Buyers usually meet this limit in one of three ways:

  • the drawing shows a contour or shoulder detail the current 80° insert cannot reproduce;
  • the part needs a separate profiling tool added to the job, and the extra change costs more time than the insert decision saves;
  • a finishing pass must follow a shape, not just size a diameter.

The trade is durability, and it is real: the narrower corner has less material supporting it, so it is less forgiving of interruptions, scale and heavy stock removal. Using a DNMG as a roughing insert because it happens to be in the holder is the mirror image of the common CNMG mistake — the corner was never the problem, the job assigned to it was.

Can DNMG handle general turning and facing too?

It can turn — a DNMG is a working turning insert, not a specialty tool that only profiles. On light and medium general work, and especially where the same insert must also finish a contour, many shops run DNMG as their only rhombic shape and accept the weaker corner as the price of one-tool simplicity.

But be honest about the two limits:

  1. 1. Facing and heavier work favour CNMG. When the job is mostly straight turning and facing on rigid parts, the D shape gives up corner strength and gains nothing — the access it exists for is never used.
  2. 2. Interrupted and scaled surfaces punish the 55° tip first. If your everyday work includes cast or forged surfaces with interruptions, trial results will usually push you back to CNMG for the roughing stage, with DNMG kept for the profile and finish.

A common mixed setup is exactly that split: CNMG for the general and roughing work, DNMG for the contour and finishing work, each in its own holder. The shapes complement each other; they are not two answers to the same job.

Does the choice depend on steel, stainless or cast iron?

The shape choice and the material choice are two separate decisions — do not let one hide the other.

  • The shape (C or D) decides corner strength and access: 80° for strength and general work, 55° for reach and profiling.
  • The material group — P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron — decides which grade range the insert should come from.
  • The chipbreaker, named by the manufacturer-specific suffix after the ISO code, decides how the chip forms and breaks in that material.

So a buyer profiling a stainless part (group M) still chooses the shape on the logic above — the contour points to DNMG — and then has the grade and chipbreaker matched to group M. At CarbideAxis that chipbreaker and grade match is confirmed manually, job by job, against your workpiece material and operation before we quote; it is never picked from a table and never assumed from the ISO code alone.

Can you swap DNMG and CNMG in the same holder?

No — and this is the most expensive misunderstanding in this comparison.

The examples in the table share a 12.7 mm inscribed circle and a 5.16 mm hole, which tempts buyers to assume the seats are the same. They are not. The pocket of a holder is cut for one shape: an 80° rhombic insert does not seat correctly in a 55° pocket, or the reverse, and the thickness also differs between the common sizes — 4.76 mm for CNMG120408 against 6.35 mm for DNMG150608. An insert that does not seat fully in its pocket is not clamped the way the holder was designed to clamp it, whatever the screw seems to say.

Changing shape therefore means changing the holder system, not just the box of inserts. That holder cost belongs in the decision from the start:

  • If you already run CNMG holders and your parts are straight turning and facing, staying with CNMG is usually the right economics as well as the right geometry.
  • If a part genuinely needs the 55° reach, budget for the D-shape holder as part of the change, and trial the insert in its proper holder — a trial in the wrong seat proves nothing.
  • If you are setting up a new station, choose the shape by the parts first and buy the holder to match, not the other way round.

Which one should you stock or trial first?

Start from the parts, not the catalogue:

  • Mostly diameters, faces and general work, some of it heavy or interrupted → trial CNMG first.
  • Contours, profiles, shoulder details, one tool to finish a shape → trial DNMG first.
  • A mix of both, in volume → stock both, rough and face with CNMG, profile and finish with DNMG.

Both example codes in this article — CNMG120408 and DNMG150608 — belong to our regularly stocked turning range, and a trial starts from 10 pieces per model: one box of a single model, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. That lets you trial the shape you do not run yet against the one you do, one box at a time, in its proper holder.

FAQ

Is DNMG weaker than CNMG?

At the corner, yes. The 55° tip has less carbide behind the cutting point than the 80° CNMG corner, so it tolerates heavy cuts and interrupted surfaces less well. That weakness is the price of its reach — it is a geometry trade, not a quality difference between the two inserts.

Does DNMG have fewer usable edges than CNMG?

No. Both are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges. Corner angle changes strength and access, not edge count — edge count is set by the shape and clearance letters earlier in the code.

Can DNMG replace CNMG in the same holder?

No. The holder pocket is cut for one shape, and the common sizes also differ in thickness (4.76 mm for CNMG120408, 6.35 mm for DNMG150608). Even though both examples share a 12.7 mm inscribed circle and a 5.16 mm hole, changing shape means changing the holder.

Which insert is better for profiling and copy turning?

DNMG. The 55° point follows contours, reaches closer to shoulders and reproduces profile details that a wide 80° corner bridges over or cannot enter. For shaped parts it often removes the need for a separate profiling tool.

Which insert is better for facing?

CNMG. Facing is general work on a flat surface where access is not the limit, so the stronger 80° corner and the turning-and-facing holder arrangement built around it are the natural fit.

Which insert should I choose for interrupted cuts?

CNMG. An interrupted cut repeatedly impacts the corner as it enters the material, and the wider, better-supported 80° corner absorbs that impact better than a 55° tip. A DNMG used for interrupted roughing usually loses corner life even when the grade is right.

Is DNMG only for finishing?

No. DNMG handles light and medium general turning as well as profiling, and some shops run it as their only rhombic shape. It is simply not the best tool for heavy roughing, facing-led work or interrupted surfaces, where CNMG's stronger corner earns its place.

What do the first letters D and C actually stand for?

They are shape letters in the ISO code: C is a rhombic insert with an 80° corner, D is a rhombic insert with a 55° corner. The letter after it, N in both DNMG and CNMG, means 0° clearance — a negative, double-sided insert.

What should I include in a quote request for DNMG or CNMG?

The full code for each model — for example DNMG150608 or CNMG120408 — plus your holder model, and the chipbreaker suffix and grade name from your current box if you have them, or at minimum the material group (P, M or K) and the operation (roughing, general, profiling or finishing). The holder matters more than usual here, because the two shapes need different holders.
