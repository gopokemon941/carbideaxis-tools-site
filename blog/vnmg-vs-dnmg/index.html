VNMG vs DNMG: 35° or 55° — Which Profiling Insert Should You Choose?

CNMG and WNMG are not interchangeable names for the same turning insert. Start with the holder, insert shape, operation and material before selecting a replacement.

VNMG vs DNMG: 35° or 55° — Which Profiling Insert Should You Choose?

Choose DNMG (55° corner) when one insert must do profiling and general turning. Choose VNMG (35° corner) only when the profile is too tight for a 55° point — fine contours, sharp shoulders and copy turning. Both are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges, but they fit different holders and are not interchangeable.

Two things in this table decide the whole comparison. First, the edge count is the same: narrowing the corner from 55° to 35° does not cost you edges — both shapes are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges. Second, the two examples do not even share a size: the stocked VNMG is a smaller insert (9.525 mm inscribed circle) than the stocked DNMG (12.7 mm), so this is never a like-for-like swap of one box for another. Shape is the first letter of the code for a reason; if you are still reading codes position by position, start with how to read an insert code.

What does the corner angle actually change?

The corner angle is the included angle at the working tip of the rhombic insert — 35° on a V shape, 55° on a D shape. Everything else in this comparison follows from that one geometric difference.

A narrower corner puts less carbide behind the cutting point. The tip is weaker, less forgiving of interruptions and heavy stock removal, and more likely to chip when the cut is imperfect. In exchange, the slim point can enter places a wider corner physically cannot: it can follow a fine contour, turn closer into a sharp shoulder, and copy a drawn profile without bridging over the detail.

That is the whole trade, and it runs in one direction. DNMG trades some access for a stronger, more versatile corner; VNMG trades almost everything for access. Neither shape is an upgrade of the other. The full dimension tables for both families are listed in the insert size chart, and both sit inside the range on the turning inserts page.

Which one is stronger, and which jobs does that suit?

DNMG, clearly — and that is why it is the default profiling shape for most shops.

For profiling that also involves real stock removal, for light and medium general turning, and for jobs where the same insert must finish a contour and then keep turning, the limiting factor is usually the corner itself. A corner that chips ends the edge long before normal wear does, and the 55° corner survives conditions that would damage a 35° tip. DNMG is also the shape to stock when you are not sure how tight the profiles will be: it reaches most contours, and where it cannot reach, the part usually needs a different approach anyway.

VNMG's strength is not strength at all — it is reach. It is the shape for the last part of a profile, the fine detail and the sharp internal corner, where a 55° point would round over the feature or leave material the drawing does not allow. Used there, on light finishing passes, it does work no other stocked negative shape can do. Used anywhere else, it is simply a weaker insert doing a job a stronger one could do.

Choosing the sharper shape does not mean choosing the finest finish the machine can take. It means that when the profile is the whole job, the corner can actually follow it.

Which one reaches better — profiling, contours and shoulders?

VNMG, and this is the only reason the shape exists in a turning range.

A 35° point can copy profiles that a 55° corner cannot follow: fine contoured surfaces, transitions into sharp shoulders, small details and finishing passes where the tool has to track a drawn profile rather than simply remove stock in straight lines. Where a DNMG would bridge over a detail or force a second tool, a VNMG often completes the profile in the same setup.

Buyers usually meet this limit in one of three ways:

  • the drawing shows a contour or shoulder detail the current 55° insert cannot reproduce;
  • the part needs a separate profiling tool added to the job, and the extra change costs more time than the insert decision saves;
  • a finishing pass must follow a shape, not just size a diameter, and the 55° corner leaves a visible step or an uncut corner.

The trade is durability, and it is real: the narrower corner has less material supporting it, so it is less forgiving of interruptions, scale and heavy stock removal. Using a VNMG as a general turning insert because it happens to be in the holder is the most common VNMG mistake — the corner was never the problem, the job assigned to it was.

Can VNMG handle general turning too?

It can turn, but it should not be your general turning insert.

On light finishing passes and on parts that are mostly profile, many shops run VNMG as a dedicated finishing shape and accept the weaker corner as the price of one-tool simplicity on that job. But be honest about the two limits:

  1. General, heavier and interrupted work favour DNMG. When the job is mostly straight turning, facing or roughing, the V shape gives up corner strength and gains nothing — the access it exists for is never used.
  2. The stocked sizes are not equal. The stocked VNMG160408 is a 9.525 mm insert; the stocked DNMG150608 is a 12.7 mm insert with a thicker body. Even before the corner angle, the DNMG brings more insert to the cut.

A common mixed setup is exactly that split: DNMG for the profiling and the general work, VNMG kept for the fine contour and finishing work, each in its own holder. The shapes complement each other; they are not two answers to the same job. For the step above DNMG — the stronger 80° corner for general and heavier turning — see DNMG vs CNMG and CNMG vs WNMG.

Does the choice depend on steel, stainless or cast iron?

The shape choice and the material choice are two separate decisions — do not let one hide the other.

  • The shape (V or D) decides corner strength and access: 55° for the versatile profiling corner, 35° for the tightest profiles.
  • The material group — P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron — decides which grade range the insert should come from.
  • The chipbreaker, named by the manufacturer-specific suffix after the ISO code, decides how the chip forms and breaks in that material.

So a buyer profiling a stainless part (group M) still chooses the shape on the logic above — the contour points to VNMG or DNMG — and then has the grade and chipbreaker matched to group M. At CarbideAxis that chipbreaker and grade match is confirmed manually, job by job, against your workpiece material and operation before we quote; it is never picked from a table and never assumed from the ISO code alone. The rake system behind both shapes is explained in negative vs positive inserts.

Can you swap VNMG and DNMG in the same holder?

No — and this is the most expensive misunderstanding in this comparison.

The holder pocket is cut for one shape: a 35° rhombic insert does not seat correctly in a 55° pocket, or the reverse. The common stocked sizes differ as well — 9.525 mm inscribed circle and 4.76 mm thickness for VNMG160408 against 12.7 mm and 6.35 mm for DNMG150608 — so even the seat dimensions do not match. An insert that does not seat fully in its pocket is not clamped the way the holder was designed to clamp it, whatever the screw seems to say.

Changing shape therefore means changing the holder system, not just the box of inserts. That holder cost belongs in the decision from the start:

  • If you already run DNMG holders and your parts are profiling and general work, staying with DNMG is usually the right economics as well as the right geometry.
  • If a part genuinely needs the 35° reach, budget for the V-shape holder (PVJN-type, against the D-shape PDJN-type) as part of the change, and trial the insert in its proper holder — a trial in the wrong seat proves nothing.
  • If you are setting up a new station, choose the shape by the parts first and buy the holder to match, not the other way round. The holder families for both shapes are listed in how to match an insert to its holder.

Which one should you stock or trial first?

Start from the parts, not the catalogue:

  • Contours, profiles and shoulder details, with real turning work around them → trial DNMG first.
  • A profile too tight for 55°, fine contours, sharp shoulders, copy turning → trial VNMG first, for that job only.
  • A mix of both, in volume → stock both, profile and turn generally with DNMG, finish the tight details with VNMG.

Both example codes in this article — VNMG160408 and DNMG150608 — belong to our regularly stocked turning range, and a trial starts from 10 pieces per model: one box of a single model, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. That lets you trial the shape you do not run yet against the one you do, one box at a time, in its proper holder. Trial orders run 7–15 days.

FAQ

Is VNMG weaker than DNMG? At the corner, yes. The 35° tip has less carbide behind the cutting point than the 55° DNMG corner, so it tolerates heavy cuts and interrupted surfaces less well. That weakness is the price of its reach — it is a geometry trade, not a quality difference between the two inserts.

Does VNMG have fewer usable edges than DNMG? No. Both are double-sided negative inserts with 4 usable edges. Corner angle changes strength and access, not edge count — edge count is set by the shape and clearance letters earlier in the code.

Can VNMG replace DNMG in the same holder? No. The holder pocket is cut for one shape, and the common stocked sizes also differ (9.525 mm inscribed circle and 4.76 mm thickness for VNMG160408, 12.7 mm and 6.35 mm for DNMG150608). Changing shape means changing the holder.

Which insert is better for profiling and copy turning? VNMG, when the profile is tight. The 35° point follows fine contours, reaches closer to sharp shoulders and reproduces profile details that a 55° corner bridges over or cannot enter. For ordinary profiling, DNMG is usually enough — and stronger.

Which insert is better for general turning? DNMG. General turning rewards the stronger 55° corner and the larger, thicker stocked insert, and the access VNMG exists for is never used on straight work.

Which insert should I choose for interrupted cuts? DNMG. An interrupted cut repeatedly impacts the corner as it enters the material, and the wider, better-supported 55° corner absorbs that impact better than a 35° tip. A VNMG used for interrupted roughing usually loses corner life even when the grade is right.

Is VNMG only for finishing? In practice, yes — profiling and finishing is where it belongs. It handles light finishing passes well, but it is not the tool for heavy roughing, general turning or interrupted surfaces, where DNMG's stronger corner earns its place.

What do the first letters V and D actually stand for? They are shape letters in the ISO code: V is a rhombic insert with a 35° corner, D is a rhombic insert with a 55° corner. The letter after it, N in both VNMG and DNMG, means 0° clearance — a negative, double-sided insert.

What should I include in a quote request for VNMG or DNMG? The full code for each model — for example VNMG160408 or DNMG150608 — plus your holder model, and the chipbreaker suffix and grade name from your current box if you have them, or at minimum the material group (P, M or K) and the operation (roughing, general, profiling or finishing). The holder matters more than usual here, because the two shapes need different holders.
