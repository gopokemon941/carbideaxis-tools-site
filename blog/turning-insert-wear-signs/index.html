Turning Insert Wear Signs — How to Read a Used Edge Before It Costs You a Part

A used edge tells you what went wrong before the next part does — if you know which sign you are looking at.

Have a turning insert code or holder photo?Check my turning insert

A used turning insert tells you what went wrong before the next part goes wrong: a dull, shiny band on the flank means normal wear reaching the end of that edge; a crater behind the cutting edge, a chipped corner, a built-up edge, or cracks across the edge each point to a different cause. The check takes a minute — look at the edge under good light, compare it with a new insert of the same code, and change only the edge, the seating, or the insert choice that matches the sign you see.

Read the table as a starting point, not a verdict. Two signs often appear together — for example, a chipped corner on an edge that was already heavily worn on the flank — and in that case the first sign (the flank wear) is the one to act on: the edge was already due for a change. The sections below take each sign in turn, with what to look for and what to photograph if you want us to look at it with you.

Is flank wear the sign that the edge is finished?

Yes, in most shops flank wear is the normal end of an edge. As the insert cuts, the flank — the face below the cutting edge — rubs against the freshly cut workpiece surface, and a shiny band grows along the edge. The edge itself stays straight, the nose radius keeps its shape, and the part surface and size drift slowly rather than failing suddenly. That slow drift is the useful part: it gives you time to change the edge on your schedule, between parts, instead of in the middle of one.

The check is a comparison, not a measurement. Hold the used insert next to a new insert of the same code under the same light and look at the flank. On the new insert the flank is a clean, uniform face; on a worn insert a bright band stands out along the cutting edge, and the edge looks rounded rather than sharp. If the band is narrow and the edge still cuts cleanly, the edge still has life in it. If the band is wide, the edge feels blunt to a fingertip (gently — do not run a finger along a sharp edge), and the part surface is starting to look dull or torn, the edge is done. Turn the insert to a fresh edge and keep the worn one for a day: it is the reference that tells you how long an edge lasts on your job, which is worth more than any general claim about tool life.

Flank wear grows faster on some jobs than others — hard workpiece skin, abrasive material and long continuous cuts all wear the flank sooner — but the action is the same in every case: change the edge, do not change the insert choice. If a fresh edge wears the same way in the same short time, then the question is no longer wear, it is whether the insert shape, nose radius and grade group fit the material and operation. That is a selection question, covered in DNMG vs CNMG and negative vs positive inserts, not a wear question.

What does a crater behind the cutting edge mean?

A crater is a hollow worn into the rake face — the top face the chip slides across — just behind the cutting edge. You see it as a dip or a dull patch a short distance back from the edge, and in a bad case the edge itself looks thin, as if it has been hollowed out from behind. The chip is the cause: as it flows across the rake face it rubs the same track, and over time that track wears into a crater.

A shallow crater on an edge that has already cut a long time is normal wear, and the action is the same as for flank wear — turn to a fresh edge. A deep crater that appears early, while the flank still looks fresh, is a different message: the chip is not breaking and flowing the way the chipbreaker was meant to guide it. The first check is the chipbreaker and nose radius match for your material and operation: a chipbreaker made for finishing will not control the chip of a heavy roughing cut, and a small nose radius on a heavy cut leaves the edge with too little support. We confirm the chipbreaker manually for each job before quoting, rather than fixing a chipbreaker to a model on a page — so if a crater keeps coming back early, send the used insert photo with the material group (P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron) and the operation, and we will check the match from the photo.

Do not confuse a crater with a chip in the edge. A crater is a smooth hollow behind the edge; a chip is a piece missing from the edge itself, covered in the next section. They have different causes and different fixes.

Why did the corner chip?

Chipping is the most visible wear sign and the easiest to misread. A small piece is missing from the nose or the cutting edge, and the break is irregular — not a smooth band like flank wear and not a hollow like a crater. The edge met something harder than the cut itself: an interrupted cut (a keyway, a hole, a cast surface), hard scale on the workpiece skin, or a hard spot in the material. The insert did not wear out; it was hit.

Before you blame the insert, check three things in this order. First, the workpiece surface: is there scale, a hard skin, or an interruption exactly where the chip happened? Second, the seating: is the insert sitting flat in the pocket, is the clamp tight, and is the holder the right one for the insert — the three-match check in how to match an insert to its holder applies here, because an insert that is not seated firmly chips far more easily. Third, the edge that chipped: was it already heavily worn on the flank before it chipped? A worn edge is a weaker edge, and a chip on a worn edge is often just the end of that edge arriving suddenly, not a new problem.

If the same corner chips on a fresh edge, on the first part, the question moves from wear to selection: the nose radius, the insert shape and the grade group may not fit an interrupted cut on that material. Send a photo of the chipped edge with the material and the operation, and we will check the match before the next trial — a trial in the same wrong combination proves nothing, and a 10-piece trial is for testing a fit, not for repeating a chip. The trial itself starts from 10 pieces per model, one box of a single model, as for every stocked model on the turning inserts page.

What is built-up edge, and why does it ruin the finish?

Built-up edge is workpiece material welded onto the cutting edge. You see it as a lump or a rough patch stuck to the edge, and the part surface it leaves behind is torn and rough rather than smooth — the lump, not the insert, is doing the cutting. It appears most often on materials that weld easily, and it comes and goes: the lump grows, breaks off, and grows again, so the finish changes from part to part without any change you made.

The check is the material group first. Stainless steel (M group) and some steels (P group) are the usual cases; cast iron (K group) rarely builds up this way. If the material group and the insert choice do not fit — for example, a grade group chosen for cast iron used on stainless steel — built-up edge is the expected result, not a surprise. The fix is a selection fix: the grade group and chipbreaker for the material, confirmed manually for the job, not a harder push on the same edge. Changing the edge alone will not help, because the new edge will build up the same way.

One thing built-up edge is not: it is not flank wear. Flank wear is the insert wearing away; built-up edge is material adding on. If you mistake one for the other you will change edges all day and the finish will stay torn. Look at the edge under good light: a built-up edge stands proud of the edge as a lump, while flank wear is a flat shiny band below it.

What is notch wear at the depth-of-cut line?

Notch wear is a groove worn into the edge exactly where the cut ends — at the depth-of-cut line, where the edge meets the uncut workpiece surface. Every other part of the edge can look fresh while one spot is worn into a notch. The cause is usually at that line: a hard skin on the workpiece, a previous cut line, or a burr that the edge meets at the same place on every pass.

The check is to look at where the notch sits. Measure nothing — just note whether the groove lines up with the surface of the workpiece where the cut ends. If it does, the wear is telling you about the workpiece surface at that line, not about the insert as a whole. If the notch appears on the first part, check the surface condition before the next edge; if it appears only after many parts, it is the edge reaching the end of its life at its hardest-working spot, and turning to a fresh edge is the right action.

Notch wear is also the sign that most often gets confused with chipping. A notch is a smooth groove at one place on the edge; a chip is an irregular break that can sit anywhere on the edge. Photograph the edge straight on, with the notch in the middle of the frame, if you want us to tell the two apart from a photo.

Why are there cracks across the edge?

Fine cracks across the cutting edge — a network of short lines, like a dry riverbed — are thermal cracks. The edge heats up in the cut and cools between cuts, and when that heating and cooling repeats sharply enough, the edge surface cracks. You will see this most on interrupted cuts, where the edge is in and out of the cut, and on jobs where coolant reaches the edge in bursts rather than steadily.

The first check is coolant delivery: is coolant reaching the cutting edge continuously, or does it arrive in splashes and miss the edge on some passes? The second check is whether the cut is interrupted — a keyway, a hole or a cast surface that makes the edge heat and cool on every revolution. Neither check is a reason to change the insert code on its own; both are reasons to look at the job setup before the next edge. If the cracks appear together with chipping, the cracks came first: a cracked edge is a weakened edge, and the chip is the crack breaking through.

Thermal cracks are a reason to stop using that edge immediately, even if the flank still looks acceptable. A cracked edge can break without warning in the next cut, and a broken edge can damage the part and the holder. Turn to a fresh edge, keep the cracked insert as the record of what happened, and send its photo with the operation if the pattern repeats.

The edge looks fine but the part is out of tolerance — what now?

This is the quietest failure in the list, and the one that wastes the most parts. The edge shows no chip, no crater and no obvious band, yet the part measures out of tolerance or the finish has gone dull. The edge has worn past its useful life without breaking: the nose radius has worn, the flank band has grown, and the cutting point has moved just enough to change the size.

The check is the side-by-side comparison, and it is the only check that works here. Put the used insert next to a new insert of the same code — the same code matters, because a different nose radius changes the size on its own — and look at the nose under good light. On the new insert the nose is a clean, regular curve; on the worn insert the curve is flattened or uneven. The difference is small, which is why the part drifted before the edge looked worn. The size chart for the code you are using is in the insert size chart: for example, CNMG120408 and CNMG120404 share the same body — 12.7 mm inscribed circle, 4.76 mm thick, 5.16 mm hole — and differ only in nose radius, 0.8 mm against 0.4 mm, so a worn 0.8 mm nose that measures like a different radius is no longer the insert you ordered. The radius comparison itself is in CNMG120408 vs CNMG120404.

The lesson is a habit, not a fix: do not judge an edge by looks alone, and do not wait for a visible break to change it. Change the edge when the part tells you to — size drifting, finish dulling — and keep a used edge from each job as the reference for how long an edge lasts on that job. That reference is what turns the next trial into a planned change instead of a surprise.

What if the insert moves in the pocket?

An insert that moves, rattles or sits unevenly in the pocket is not wearing out — it is not seated. The signs are a shiny spot on the bottom of the insert where it has rubbed the pocket, a clamp that feels loose, or an edge that chips on the first cut for no visible reason. Stop and check the seating before you change anything else: a loose insert will chip, wear unevenly and measure badly, and no insert choice fixes a seating problem.

Check the pocket for chips and dirt, check the clamp and screw for wear, and check that the holder is the right family for the insert — the shape, size and rake-system checks in how to match an insert to its holder are the same checks. The most common cause is the simplest: a chip or dirt in the pocket from the last insert change, or a clamp screw that was not tightened fully. Clean the pocket, seat a fresh edge, and confirm the clamp before the next cut.

How to photograph a worn insert so we can read it with you

You do not need a microscope — a phone photo in good light is enough if it shows the right things. Photograph the insert from three sides: the rake face (top, showing any crater), the flank (side, showing the wear band), and the nose straight on (showing any chip or notch). Put a new insert of the same code in one photo next to the used one for scale and comparison, and include the box or the code stamped on the insert so we know the exact model.

Send the photos with three facts: the workpiece material (or the material group P / M / K), the operation (roughing, general turning, boring or finishing), and how many parts the edge cut before you noticed the sign. We reply with identification and matching within 1 working day through our photo identification service, and we confirm the chipbreaker manually for your job before quoting. You do not need to decide which wear sign it is before you send the photo — naming the sign is our part of the check.

A short checklist for the next edge change

  • Look at the flank first: a straight, even shiny band is normal wear — turn to a fresh edge
  • Look behind the edge: a hollow on the rake face is a crater — check the chipbreaker and nose radius match
  • Look at the corner: an irregular missing piece is a chip — check the workpiece surface and the seating
  • Look for lumps on the edge: material stuck to the edge is built-up edge — check the material group
  • Look at the depth-of-cut line: a groove at one spot is notch wear — check the surface at that line
  • Look for cracks across the edge: a cracked edge stops now, even if the flank looks acceptable
  • Compare with a new insert of the same code before you decide the edge is still good — size drift comes before a visible break
  • Keep one used insert from each job as your reference for how long an edge lasts on that job

If two signs appear together, act on the first one — the flank wear that made the edge weak, or the seating problem that made it chip. And if the same sign comes back on a fresh edge, on the first part, stop repeating the trial and send the photo: the answer is in the match, not in another edge of the same combination.

FAQ

How do I tell normal flank wear from a problem?

Normal flank wear is an even, shiny band along the flank with the edge still straight and the nose still round. It means the edge is near the end of its life — turn to a fresh edge. A problem shows as a crater behind the edge, a chip in the corner, material stuck to the edge, a groove at one spot, or cracks across the edge, and each of those points to a different cause in the table above.

How long should a turning insert edge last?

There is no single number that fits every job — it depends on the material, the operation, the nose radius and whether the cut is interrupted. The useful measure is your own reference: keep one used insert from each job and note how many parts its edges cut. That reference tells you when to change the next edge on the same job, which no general claim can replace.

Can I keep using an insert with a small chip in the corner?

No. A chipped corner leaves a broken edge in the cut, damages the part surface, and can break further without warning. Turn to a fresh edge, and check the workpiece surface and the insert seating before the next edge — a chip on a fresh edge on the first part is a selection or seating question, not a wear question.

What causes a crater on the rake face?

Chips rubbing the same track across the rake face wear a hollow behind the cutting edge. A shallow crater on an old edge is normal wear; a deep crater that appears early, while the flank still looks fresh, usually means the chipbreaker and nose radius do not fit the material and operation, and the chip is not breaking and flowing as intended.

What is built-up edge, and how do I stop it?

Built-up edge is workpiece material welded onto the cutting edge, leaving a torn finish. It is a material-match problem, most often on stainless steel and some steels: check the material group (P / M / K) and whether the insert choice fits it. Changing the edge alone will not help — the new edge will build up the same way.

Why does the edge wear in a groove at the depth-of-cut line?

That is notch wear: a hard skin, a previous cut line or a burr at the exact place where the cut ends wears one spot on the edge. Check the workpiece surface condition at that line. If it appears only after many parts, it is the edge reaching the end of its life at its hardest-working spot — turn to a fresh edge.

Are cracks across the edge dangerous?

Yes. Fine thermal cracks mean the edge is heating and cooling in sharp cycles, usually on interrupted cuts or with uneven coolant delivery. A cracked edge can break without warning in the next cut, so stop using it immediately, turn to a fresh edge, and keep the cracked insert as the record.

My insert looks fine but parts are drifting out of tolerance — why?

The nose radius has worn and the cutting point has moved, without any visible break. Compare the used insert with a new insert of the same code side by side under good light: the worn nose looks flattened or uneven. Change the edge when the part tells you to — size drifting or finish dulling — not when the edge looks broken.

What should I photograph to get help reading a worn insert?

Three views — the rake face from above, the flank from the side, and the nose straight on — plus one photo with a new insert of the same code next to the used one, and the box or code so we know the exact model. Send them with the material group, the operation, and how many parts the edge cut, through our photo identification service, and we reply with identification and matching within 1 working day.

Not sure what your used edge is telling you?

Send us photos of the worn insert — rake face, flank and nose — with the material and the operation. We will read the wear sign, confirm the chipbreaker manually for your job, and quote from there. One photo set is enough: you do not need to name the wear sign before you send it.
