What Photos to Send for Insert Matching: The 5 Views That Identify Your Insert

Send five views: the box label, the top of the insert, its side profile, the bottom face, and the holder marking with the empty seat. Add a close-up of the worn corner if you are replacing a failed insert. Phone photos in good light are enough — we reply with a preliminary identification within one working day, and the match is confirmed before quoting.

The 5 views at a glance

PhotoWhat it showsWhy it matters
Box labelFull code, grade and chipbreaker suffix as printedThe fastest identification there is — the label answers most questions before the insert is even looked at
Top of the insert (rake face)Shape, corner angle, chipbreaker formShape letter and corner angle narrow the code to one or two families
Side profileThickness and clearance angleSeparates negative (0°) from positive (7°) inserts and confirms the size class
Bottom faceSeat side, hole and any marking stamped thereConfirms the fixing type and whether the insert is single- or double-sided

Photo 1: the box label — always first

If you still have the box, photograph the label before the insert itself. The label carries the complete code — shape, size, nose radius — plus the two things the insert cannot tell us by itself: the grade name and the chipbreaker suffix your current supplier uses. Those two decide how the insert behaves in your material, and they are maker-specific, so a replacement matched on shape alone can still cut differently.

Photograph the whole label flat, in focus, with no glare across the text. If the box is gone but the insert marking is readable, the top-face photo below does the same job.

Photo 2: the top of the insert

Place the insert on a plain, light surface and photograph it straight from above. This view shows the shape — 80° diamond, trigon, triangle, square — and the corner angle, which is the first letter of the code. It also shows the chipbreaker form pressed into the rake face, and any code stamped on the insert itself.

Keep one insert per photo. A pile of inserts tells us you have many; a single insert tells us which one. If the corner you actually run is worn, turn that corner toward the camera — the worn corner is information, not a flaw in the photo.

Photo 3: the side profile

Stand the insert on its edge, or hold it between two fingers with the camera level with it, and photograph the side. The profile shows two things no top view can: the thickness, and the clearance angle below the cutting edge. A flank that drops straight down means a negative insert (0° clearance, usable on both sides); a flank that leans away means a positive insert (7° clearance, one side only). That single distinction halves the possibilities, and it is the check buyers most often skip. Why the two systems need different holders is covered in negative vs positive inserts.

Photo 4: the bottom face

Turn the insert over and photograph the face that sits in the pocket. This confirms the fixing hole, shows whether the insert is truly double-sided, and catches markings that some makers stamp on the underside. It also shows the seat contact surface — if the bottom is shiny in one spot or damaged, that is a seating problem worth knowing about before a replacement is blamed for it, the same check as in how to match an insert to its holder.

Photo 5: the holder marking and the empty seat

An insert does not have to fit a catalogue; it has to fit your holder. Photograph the code stamped on the holder shank — five letters that name the clamping, the insert shape it takes, the approach angle, the clearance system and the hand — and take one photo of the empty seat with the insert removed. The seat photo shows the pocket shape and the clamp, and it settles questions the insert photos cannot: a correct insert in the wrong pocket is the most expensive photo never taken. If the marking has worn off, the seat photo plus the insert photos usually still identify the family.

Replacing a failed insert? Add the worn corner

If you are not just reordering but replacing an insert that chipped, wore out early or ruined a finish, add one close-up of the failed corner, as straight-on and as close as your phone focuses. The wear pattern is the diagnosis: flank wear, a crater, chipping and built-up edge each point to a different cause and a different first check, as set out in turning insert wear signs. A replacement chosen without reading the old edge can repeat the same failure in a new box.

What if you have several models to match?

One set of photos per model, in the same order — label, top, side, bottom, holder — and a short list of the codes and quantities you already know. Sets that arrive mixed force us to guess which top belongs to which bottom, and guessing is what this page exists to avoid. Send the sets one after another in the same WhatsApp conversation and number them if you can; the matching work is ours, but the sorting has to happen at your end, where the inserts actually are. The full range these photos get matched against is on the turning inserts page.

Four things that make a photo unusable

  • Blur. Tap to focus on the insert, not the bench. If the code letters are not readable to you, they are not readable to us.
  • Darkness. Photograph near a window or under a white work light. A flashlight from the side is better than a camera flash from the front, which whites out the rake face.
  • Fingers over the corner. Hold the insert by its sides. The corner hidden under a thumb is usually the corner that decides the code.
  • No scale. Add a coin or a ruler in one frame. Shape alone can mislead between two sizes of the same family; a scale reference settles it instantly.

What happens after you send the photos

The photos go to our photo identification service. You receive a preliminary identification first — the most likely ISO code direction from the shape, size and seat evidence — within one working day. Then the chipbreaker and grade match for your workpiece material and operation is confirmed manually, and only then is anything quoted. A photo never produces a price by itself: it produces an identification you can check, and a quotation built on a confirmed match. If you can read part of the code yourself, the insert code guide shows what each position means and which digits to double-check.

FAQ

Can you identify an insert from just one photo?

Sometimes — a clear box label alone is often enough. But a single photo of the insert itself usually narrows the code to a family, not a full match: the side profile is what separates negative from positive, and the label is what carries the grade and chipbreaker. Five views take two minutes and remove the guesswork.

Do I need a microscope or special camera?

No. A phone camera in good light is enough for identification. What matters is focus, light and the right views — not magnification. If you have a caliper, adding the inscribed circle and thickness in your message speeds things up, but it is an extra, not a requirement.

Should I clean the insert before photographing it?

Wipe off oil and loose chips with a cloth so the shape and markings show. Do not grind, polish or wire-brush the edge: the wear pattern on a used insert is evidence, and cleaning it away removes the information a replacement decision needs.

The code on my insert has worn off completely. Is it still worth sending photos?

Yes — that is exactly the case photos exist for. Shape, corner angle, thickness, clearance and the holder seat still identify the family and size class from the insert itself. Identification stays preliminary until the match is confirmed with you, but a worn-off code is a starting point, not a dead end.

Can you quote a price from the photos alone?

No. Photos produce a preliminary identification; the quotation comes after the chipbreaker and grade match is confirmed against your material and operation. That order protects you: a price given on an unconfirmed match is a price for the wrong insert surprisingly often.

How many inserts should be in each photo?

One. A single insert per frame, one model per set of five views. Group photos are fine as an extra that shows quantity, but the identification views should each show exactly one insert.

The insert is still mounted in the holder. Do I have to remove it?

For the top view and the holder marking, no. For the side profile and the bottom face, yes — those two views need the insert out. If removing it is not possible right now, send what you can and say so; a partial set still starts the identification.

What if my holder marking is worn off too?

Photograph the empty seat and the clamp from above and from the side. Pocket shape and clamp type identify the holder family in most cases, and combined with the insert photos the match can usually be confirmed.

How fast will I hear back?

The preliminary identification comes within one working day. If the match needs a detail only you can supply — a material, an operation, a clearer view of one corner — we ask for that one thing rather than guessing.

Not sure which code you are running?

Send the five views — label, top, side, bottom, holder — on WhatsApp or through the photo identification service. You get a preliminary identification within one working day, a confirmed match before any quotation, and no obligation to order the trial that follows from it.
