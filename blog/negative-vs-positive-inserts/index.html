Negative vs Positive Turning Inserts: Which Rake Should You Choose?

The second letter of the ISO code — N or C — decides edges per insert, cutting force and which holder system you need.

Have a turning insert code or holder photo?Check my turning insert

Choose a negative insert (for example CNMG120408) when the machine is rigid, the cut is heavy or interrupted, and you want the most usable edges per insert. Choose a positive insert (for example CCMT09T308) when the machine is small, the setup is less rigid, or you are boring a small hole — the sharper geometry cuts with lower force. The second letter of the ISO code tells you which one you are holding: N means 0° clearance, C means 7° clearance.

One line in this table decides more purchases than any other: a negative insert is double-sided and a positive insert is single-sided. Everything else — strength, force, machine size, cost per edge — follows from that one difference. If you are still reading codes position by position, start with how to read an insert code.

What does "negative" actually mean?

"Negative" describes the clearance angle, not the quality of the insert — and it is the second letter of the ISO code.

A negative insert has 0° clearance: the flank of the insert stands vertical to the workpiece. To cut at all, the holder tilts the insert so the edge meets the material at a negative rake angle. The edge is therefore backed by a large wedge of carbide, which is why it is strong. The price is cutting force: pushing a blunter wedge through metal takes more force, and that force has to go somewhere — into the machine, the holder and the workpiece.

A positive insert has a clearance angle built in — 7° for the C in CCMT and DCMT. The insert already leans away from the workpiece, so the edge meets the material at a positive rake and slices rather than pushes. Cutting force drops, which is exactly what a small lathe, a long boring bar or a thin-walled part needs. The price is the edge itself: with clearance on only one side, the insert can only be used on that side, and the sharper edge has less carbide behind it.

This is why the same shop often runs both. The letters N and C are not a ranking; they are two answers to two different constraints — rigidity and force on one side, edge strength on the other. The full dimension tables for both families are listed in the insert size chart, and both sit inside the range on the turning inserts page.

Why do negative inserts give you more edges?

Because both faces are usable.

A negative insert is flat on both sides — 0° clearance means there is no "top" and "bottom" in the cutting sense. Flip it over and the other face cuts exactly the same way. So the edge count doubles: a CNMG has 4 usable edges, a WNMG has 6, a TNMG has 6, and an SNMG has 8. That is the whole economics of the negative family — the insert costs more per piece than a single positive insert of the same size, but the cost is spread over twice as many edges. Work it out per edge, not per piece, with the cost-per-edge calculator — the SNMG at 8 edges is the cheapest per edge in our turning range on that maths alone.

A positive insert is single-sided by geometry. The 7° clearance only works one way up: turn it over and the clearance faces the wrong way, so the insert cannot cut. A CCMT or DCMT therefore has 2 usable edges, a triangular positive insert has 3. You pay for one side and you use one side — which is a fair trade when the job needs the lower cutting force, and a poor trade when it does not.

Two warnings follow from this. First, never compare a negative and a positive insert by piece price alone — the edge counts differ by a factor of two or more, so the cheaper box can easily be the dearer edge. Second, never flip a positive insert hoping for two more edges; the used side is the only side, and a flipped positive insert does not seat or cut the way its holder expects.

Which one is stronger?

The negative insert, clearly — and that is why it is the default for general turning.

The negative edge is a thick wedge with a full block of carbide behind it. It tolerates heavy cuts, scale on cast surfaces, hard spots and interrupted cuts — the entry and exit of the edge against the material — better than a sharp positive edge, because there is simply more material supporting the point that takes the impact. That is why our general-purpose turning range is built around negative inserts: CNMG120408 for everyday external turning and facing, WNMG080408 where six edges matter, SNMG120408 for roughing. The shape-by-shape differences inside the negative family are covered in CNMG vs WNMG and DNMG vs CNMG.

The positive edge is weaker by design. A sharp edge with 7° clearance has less carbide behind it, so the same impact that a negative corner absorbs can chip a positive corner. Using a positive insert for heavy roughing because it happened to be in the holder is one of the most common causes of early edge failure — the insert was never the wrong quality, it was given the wrong job.

Strength, however, is not the whole decision. A strong edge that the machine cannot push is no use at all, which is the next question.

When does a positive insert win?

Whenever cutting force is the limiting factor rather than edge strength.

The clearest cases are the ones buyers already know by feel: a small lathe that stalls or chatters on a negative insert, a long boring bar that flexes, a thin-walled part that deflects under the cut, a small-diameter internal bore where there is no room for a rigid setup. In each of these the positive insert's lower cutting force is not a nice-to-have — it is what makes the cut possible at all. That is why the positive family in our standard range — CCMT09T308, CCMT09T304, CCMT060204 and DCMT11T308 — is listed for steel (P) and stainless (M) on smaller lathes and boring work, and why the shape-by-shape differences inside that family are covered in CCMT vs DCMT vs TCMT.

A second case is finishing work where surface quality matters and the cut is light anyway. A sharp positive edge takes a light finishing pass cleanly, and the strength it gives up is strength the job never needed.

The rule of thumb is simple: if the machine, the bar or the part is the weak link, choose positive; if the edge is the weak link, choose negative. Most shops discover this the expensive way — by chipping positive inserts on heavy work, or by chattering negative inserts on a small machine — and then keep both families in stock.

Can you use a negative insert on a small machine?

You can try, but the machine will tell you quickly, and the answer is usually no.

A negative insert needs the machine to push a blunt wedge through metal. On a rigid lathe with enough power that force is unremarkable. On a small or older lathe the same force shows up as chatter, deflection, poor surface finish, or the machine simply struggling to hold size. The insert is not at fault — the force was always part of the negative design, and the machine was never built to supply it.

The honest test is a trial, not a catalogue claim. Trial one box of the positive equivalent — same shape family where possible, same nose radius — on the same part, and compare the finish and the stability rather than the insert price. Both example codes in this article belong to our regularly stocked turning range, and a trial starts from 10 pieces per model: one box of a single model, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. That lets you trial the geometry you do not run yet against the one you do, one box at a time, in its proper holder.

Do negative and positive inserts fit the same holder?

No — and this is the mistake that costs a holder, not just an insert.

A holder is built for one rake system. A negative holder tilts a flat, double-sided insert into its negative working angle and clamps it from above; a positive holder seats a single-sided insert with its built-in clearance facing the right way. The pockets, the clamp and the screw position are all different, so an insert from one system does not seat correctly in the other — even when the inscribed circle looks similar. CCMT09T308 and CNMG120408, for example, are different sizes as well as different systems (9.525 mm against 12.7 mm inscribed circle), so there is no overlap to exploit.

Changing rake therefore means changing the holder system, not just the box of inserts — the same lesson as changing shape in DNMG vs CNMG. Budget for the holder as part of the change, and trial the insert in its proper holder: a trial in the wrong seat proves nothing about the insert. The three checks that decide whether an insert seats in a holder are in how to match an insert to its holder.

How should you choose — a short decision path

Start from the constraint, not the catalogue:

  • Rigid machine, general or heavy turning, interrupted or scaled surfaces → negative first (CNMG for general work, SNMG for roughing, WNMG where edge count matters).
  • Small machine, long boring bar, thin wall, small internal bore, light finishing → positive first (CCMT for general small work, DCMT for profiling and internal features).
  • A mix of both, in volume → stock both families, rough and face with negative, bore and finish light work with positive.

Then, separately, choose the shape for the part (see the shape comparisons linked above), the material group — P for steel, M for stainless steel, K for cast iron — for the grade range, and the chipbreaker for the material and operation. Shape, rake, material group and chipbreaker are four separate decisions; letting one hide another is how buyers end up with the right shape in the wrong system. At CarbideAxis that chipbreaker and grade match is confirmed manually, job by job, against your workpiece material and operation before we quote; it is never picked from a table and never assumed from the ISO code alone.

FAQ

What is the difference between negative and positive inserts?

The clearance angle, shown by the second letter of the ISO code. Negative inserts (N, 0°) are double-sided and sit at a negative rake in the holder, giving a strong edge and more usable edges but higher cutting force. Positive inserts (C, 7°) are single-sided with a sharper edge and lower cutting force, suited to smaller machines and boring work.

How do I tell if an insert is negative or positive?

Read the second letter of the ISO code: N means 0° clearance (negative), C means 7° clearance (positive). CNMG, WNMG, TNMG, SNMG and DNMG are negative; CCMT and DCMT are positive. The full decoding is in how to read an insert code.

Are negative inserts stronger than positive inserts?

Yes, at the edge. The negative edge is backed by more carbide and tolerates heavy cuts, scale and interrupted surfaces better. The positive edge is sharper and cuts with less force, but has less material behind it and chips more easily under heavy or interrupted cutting.

Why do negative inserts have more cutting edges?

Because they are double-sided: 0° clearance means both faces cut the same way, so flipping the insert gives a fresh set of edges. CNMG has 4, WNMG and TNMG have 6, SNMG has 8. Positive inserts are single-sided, so CCMT and DCMT have 2 usable edges.

Can I use a positive insert for roughing?

Not as a rule. A positive edge is designed for light cuts, small machines and finishing, and heavy roughing usually chips it early. If the job is mostly roughing on a rigid machine, a negative insert is the right starting point; keep the positive insert for the lighter work it was made for.

Can I use a negative insert on a small lathe?

Often not well. A negative insert needs more cutting force, and a small or less rigid lathe may chatter, deflect or struggle to hold size. On small machines, long boring bars and thin-walled parts, a positive insert's lower cutting force is usually the better fit — trial one box and compare the finish.

Do negative and positive inserts use the same holder?

No. Holders are built for one rake system: the pocket, clamp and screw position differ between negative and positive holders, and the insert sizes differ too. Changing rake means changing the holder system, and the holder cost belongs in the decision from the start.

Is a positive insert cheaper than a negative insert?

Per piece it can look cheaper, but that is the wrong comparison. Compare cost per usable edge: a negative insert spreads its price over 4 to 8 edges, a positive insert over 2. Run the numbers in the cost-per-edge calculator before deciding on price.

What should I include in a quote request for negative or positive inserts?

The full code for each model — for example CNMG120408 or CCMT09T308 — plus your holder model, and the chipbreaker suffix and grade name from your current box if you have them, or at minimum the material group (P, M or K) and the operation (roughing, general, boring or finishing). The holder matters more than usual here, because the two rake systems need different holders.
