How to Run a 10-Piece Insert Trial: Test One Job, Record Both Tools, Judge by Cost per Acceptable Part

Run a 10-piece insert trial on one job and one operation only. Record your current insert first, on the same job and the same conditions, then change nothing except the insert and record the trial insert the same way. Judge it by insert cost per acceptable part, not piece price. Trial terms for the 12 regular turning models are stated once, here: trial orders.

That is the whole method. Everything below is the five steps in detail, the record that supports them, and the mistakes that quietly invalidate trials that were set up with good intentions. The same framework sits behind our insert trial page, which also carries the downloadable record sheet this article follows.

Why do most insert trials prove nothing?

Because the comparison is unfair before the first part is cut.

The usual pattern looks reasonable: a box of trial inserts arrives, the operator fits one when the current edge wears out, the job happens to be a different part that week, a condition gets adjusted because the machine sounds different, and at the end someone says the new insert "felt about the same" or "didn't last". Nothing in that sentence can be checked. The two inserts never ran the same job, the conditions were not written down for either, scrap was never separated from acceptable parts, and the failure mode — the single most useful piece of evidence — was thrown away with the used edge.

A trial that proves something is just stricter: one defined job, the old tool recorded on it first, one change at a time, a written result for both. Ten pieces is plenty — a box of ten inserts, each with several usable edges, gives far more edges than a fair test needs. If the record is honest, ten pieces decide the question; if it is not, a hundred pieces decide nothing.

Step by step: how do you run the trial?

Step 1 — Fix one job and one operation

Choose the job where the question actually lives. If the current insert chips on one interrupted roughing job, that job is the trial — not a smooth finishing job where any insert looks good. Write down the job reference, the operation, and whether the cut is continuous or interrupted. One trial answers one question: does this insert beat my current insert on this job? A second operation is a second trial, run afterwards, not folded into the first.

Step 2 — Record the current insert first

Before the trial box is opened, run the insert you already use on the chosen job and record it properly: acceptable parts per edge, how many edges you evaluated, the surface result against the requirement, and how the edges failed. This baseline is the number the trial has to beat, and it is the step most often skipped — usually because the current insert's performance feels like common knowledge. It is not; it is a feeling, and feelings are exactly what the trial exists to replace. If the baseline turns out better than everyone assumed, you may not need a new insert at all — and finding that out cost one recorded run instead of a purchase.

Step 3 — Change only the insert

Fit the trial insert into the same holder, on the same machine, with the same workholding, coolant arrangement and overhang, and run the same job at the conditions you recorded for the baseline. Record the cutting speed, feed and depth of cut you actually use, with their units, for both tools — the point is that they are written down and the same, not that they match any recommendation. If a condition genuinely has to change — the trial insert cannot run the job as set up — change it, but write down what changed and treat the result as a different comparison: insert plus new conditions against the old baseline. That can still be useful information. What it cannot do is tell you what the insert alone did.

Step 4 — Record edge by edge, old and new separately

Do not summarise from memory at the end of the week. For each tool, separately, note how many acceptable parts each evaluated edge produced, how many parts were scrapped, the surface finish target and the measured result, and the failure mode of each edge — normal wear, chipping, built-up edge, breakage — with a photograph of the used edge where you can take one. Photographs matter because the failure mode tells you and the supplier what to change next; our guide to turning insert wear signs shows how to read what a used edge is saying. Also record cycle time and tool-change time for both tools: an insert that lasts longer but slows the cycle may not be winning, and you can only see that if both times are on the sheet.

Step 5 — Decide by cost per acceptable part, and write the conclusion

Work the formula in the next section for both tools, compare like with like, and then write three things down while the evidence is in front of you: the conclusion, what condition — if any — you changed, and the next step. A trial that ends without those three lines gets re-run from scratch six months later by someone who does not know it ever happened.

What should the trial record actually capture?

One sheet per trial, with the job described once at the top and the two tools recorded in parallel underneath. This is the layout of our free trial record sheet, which you can download from the insert trial page and use for any supplier's inserts, not only ours:

BlockFields to fill in
Job header (once)Company / job reference; trial date; operator / contact; workpiece material and hardness; machine / holder / workholding / coolant; operation and cut interruptions
Per tool — identityFull insert code, grade group, chipbreaker and batch — recorded separately for the existing tool and the trial tool
Per tool — conditions usedCutting speed and unit; feed and unit; depth of cut and unit — the values you actually ran, written down for both tools
Per tool — resultsParts per usable edge; number of edges evaluated; acceptable parts and scrap, counted separately; surface finish target and measured result; edge-failure mode and photographs; cycle time and tool-change time
Per tool — cost inputsInsert price and usable edges per insert
Closing lineRepeatable result / conditions changed / next step

Two fields on that list do more work than the rest. The first is the failure mode: "lasted 40 parts" says nothing about what to fix, while "chipped on the interrupted section, photograph attached" tells a supplier exactly which property the next candidate needs. The second is the separation of acceptable parts from scrap. A tool that produces more parts and more scrap at the same time has not necessarily improved anything, and only a record that counts them separately can show it.

How do you judge the result — cost per acceptable part?

With one formula, applied identically to both tools:

Insert cost per acceptable part = insert price / (usable edges per insert × acceptable parts per edge)

That is the same formula used on our insert trial page, and the cost-per-edge calculator will do the per-edge half of the arithmetic for you. Read the formula carefully, because every word in the denominator is doing a job. *Usable* edges — not the edges printed on the box, the edges your holder and operation actually let you use. *Acceptable* parts per edge — parts that passed, not parts that came off the machine. An insert that is cheap per piece but produces fewer acceptable parts per edge loses this calculation to a dearer insert that runs cleanly, and that is the correct result: you sell acceptable parts, not insert consumption.

A worked example, with invented numbers — these are illustration figures only, not our prices and not a quote:

Current insert (example)Trial insert (example)
Insert price7.20 per piece6.00 per piece
Usable edges per insert44
Acceptable parts per edge (as recorded)3045
Insert cost per acceptable part7.20 / (4 × 30) = 0.0606.00 / (4 × 45) = 0.033

In this example the trial insert is cheaper per piece *and* cheaper per acceptable part, so the decision is easy. The formula earns its keep in the harder case the table does not show: when the trial insert costs more per piece but lasts enough longer, per edge, to come out cheaper per acceptable part — or the reverse, when a bargain insert's short edge life makes every part dearer. Either way, the piece price alone could not have told you.

One limit, stated plainly on the trial page as well: this is the insert-cost component only. For the full production cost, add machine time, tool-change time and scrap cost — which is why the record sheet captures cycle time, tool-change time and scrap alongside the parts count. An insert that wins on insert cost but loses heavily on cycle time may not win overall, and the honest comparison says so.

What are the mistakes that ruin a trial?

MistakeWhy it ruins the result
Changing several things at once — new insert, new feed, different coolantThe improvement or failure cannot be attributed; you learn that *something* changed, not what
Judging by the first few partsA fresh edge almost always looks good; the question is what the edge looks like at the end of its life, and how it fails
Counting total parts instead of acceptable partsScrap disappears into the total and a tool that scraps more can appear to produce more
Trialing before checking the holderAn insert in the wrong pocket or the wrong holder size fails for seating reasons and tells you nothing about the insert; check the match first, as described in how to match an insert to its holder
Skipping the baseline and going straight to the trial insertThere is nothing to beat; "it ran fine" is not a comparison
Running the trial on a different, easier jobThe trial answers a question nobody asked, and the problem job is still unsolved
Recording nothing and relying on the operator's impressionImpressions cannot be re-checked, priced or handed to a supplier; the trial has to be repeated to be believed

Every row in that table is a trial that consumed inserts, machine time and goodwill and produced an opinion instead of a result. The five steps above are simply the rows of this table, inverted.

What should you send the supplier before the trial?

The trial starts before any insert is shipped, with the information that lets the supplier match a candidate to your job instead of sending a generic box. This is the checklist from our insert trial page:

  • Insert and holder — the full insert code, the grade group and chipbreaker of what you run now, and the holder model; or photographs of the insert, its box and the holder if any of that is unknown.
  • Workpiece — the material designation, hardness if known, and whether the cut is continuous or interrupted. If you describe the material by group, use the ISO group — P for steels, M for stainless steels, K for cast irons.
  • Operation and setup — roughing or finishing, the machine, the workholding, coolant and overhang.
  • Current result — acceptable parts per usable edge as you run today, the finish requirement, and the main failure mode of the current insert.
  • The trial itself — the quantity you want to trial, the destination country, and the repeat demand you expect if the trial succeeds.

Send that, and two things happen that a bare model number cannot produce. The holder can be checked before anything ships, so the trial tests the insert and not the seating; and the grade group and chipbreaker can be matched to your material and operation deliberately. On our side, that matching is confirmed manually, job by job, before we quote — never read off a table and assumed — because the same ISO code with the wrong chipbreaker for the operation is one of the most common reasons a trial insert underperforms an insert it should have beaten.

FAQ

How many inserts do I need for a fair trial? Fewer than most buyers expect. One box of 10 pieces of one model gives you ten inserts multiplied by their usable edges — far more edges than a controlled test on one job consumes. What makes a trial fair is not the quantity but the discipline: same job, same conditions, both tools recorded. Ten pieces tested loosely prove less than ten pieces tested strictly.

What is the minimum trial order at CarbideAxis? 10 pieces of one model — one box, with no need to mix models to reach a minimum. Trial orders run 7–15 days. That quantity applies to our 12 regularly stocked turning models.

Should I change the cutting conditions for the trial insert? Not at first. Run the trial insert at the conditions you recorded for your current insert, so the insert is the only difference. Record the speed, feed and depth of cut you use, with units, for both tools. If the trial insert clearly needs a different setup, change it deliberately, write down what changed, and treat the result as a comparison of insert-plus-conditions — still useful, but a different claim.

How many edges should I evaluate before deciding? Enough that the result is not one edge's luck. Evaluate several edges of each tool on the same job, record each one, and compare the pattern — parts per edge, surface result and failure mode — rather than the single best or worst edge. The record sheet asks for the number of edges evaluated precisely so a decision is never based on an unstated sample of one.

What is cost per acceptable part, and why not just compare prices? Insert cost per acceptable part = insert price / (usable edges per insert × acceptable parts per edge). Piece price ignores the two things that decide what an insert really costs you: how many of its edges you can actually use, and how many good parts each edge makes. A cheaper insert that makes fewer acceptable parts per edge costs more per part. The formula is insert cost only — add machine time, tool-change time and scrap for the full picture.

What if the trial insert fails quickly? Record how it failed before you conclude anything: the failure mode, the parts it completed, and a photograph of the edge. A fast failure with a clear mode — chipping on an interrupted section, for example — is a usable result that tells the supplier what property to change in the next candidate. A fast failure with no record is just a wasted box. Send us the record and the photographs and the matching can be revisited for the job as it actually is.

Do I need to trial on my hardest job? Trial on the job the decision is about. If you want to replace the insert on a specific problem job, that job is the test, because a trial insert that shines on easy work may change nothing where it matters. If you are stocking a general-purpose insert, trial it on a representative everyday job instead. Either way, fix the job in advance and record it — do not let the trial drift to whatever is on the machine.

Can I trial two candidate inserts at the same time? Yes, if each one gets the full discipline: the same baseline, the same job, its own separate record. What you cannot do is run them loosely in parallel on different work and compare impressions afterwards. Run one candidate properly, write the conclusion, then run the second against the same baseline if the first did not settle the question.

The full insert code and holder model — or photographs of the insert, box and holder — plus the workpiece material and hardness if known, whether the cut is continuous or interrupted, the operation and setup, your current acceptable parts per edge and main failure mode, and the trial quantity, destination country and expected repeat demand.
